# Untitled (Copy)

The work is a plain statement in a frame: *"This is a work of art. The only original thing about it is the name at the bottom."* Signed **y-a-v-a**. Try to take it — select and copy, or press **Take it** — and the browser's own `copy` event is intercepted before the clipboard is written. What lands on your clipboard is the same statement, byte-for-byte, with exactly one line rewritten: it is now signed **you**, numbered as a fresh edition. Paste it anywhere and you are the author. The page keeps a small live ledger of the theft in RAM only; reload and it never happened.

The mechanism **is** the argument (P1). Copying, on the web, is total and free — a lossless clone with no material loss to distinguish original from reproduction. So where did the aura go? Benjamin said mechanical reproduction withers it; the art market answers by relocating the entire value into the signature. This piece makes that literal: the delta between the original and your copy is a single name. Richard Prince rephotographed other people's advertisements and re-signed them into seven-figure works (*Untitled (Cowboy)*, 1980–89); Sherrie Levine re-photographed Walker Evans (*After Walker Evans*, 1981). Appropriation art's scandalous claim — that taking *is* making — is here executed by `clipboardData.setData()`: the instant you take the work, it hands you authorship (P2, P6).

It reads at two depths (P7). The casual viewer gets a clean gag: you copy the art and it cheekily credits you. The literate viewer gets the whole readymade/reproduction/aura argument compressed into one intercepted DOM event — a Duchampian gesture where the found object is the clipboard itself. The tone stays dry rather than preachy: "Originals lost: 0," the ledger deadpans, because nothing is ever lost when everything is a copy (P9).

The delivery ethics are part of the work (P8). There is no network, no storage, no cookie, no tracker; your clipboard is written **only** during your own copy gesture and is never read. The licence is deliberately **CC BY-SA 4.0** — share-alike — so the appropriation cannot be enclosed: whatever you re-sign stays exactly as copyable as the original you took it from. This is the same twenty-year argument (narrative) — *the internet already embodies the gestures of 20th-century art* — answering *COPY / COPY* and *The Original* from a new angle: not the byte-difference of a forgery, nor the fiction of scarcity, but the live moment of appropriation itself, performed by the reader in one keystroke.
