# CSS Gradient Demonstration

A full-viewport painting is generated per visit and per click, and directly beneath it sits its own complete, executable recipe: a single line of CSS, `background: linear-gradient(37deg, #… 0%, #… 42%, #… 100%);`. The painting above the label and the text of the label are not two things. The browser's paint engine reads that exact string and produces the picture; copy the string into any stylesheet on earth and you have the work again, byte-for-byte identical instruction. There is no original image file — nothing to acquire, nothing to forge, only a recipe that reproduces perfectly and costs nothing.

The work answers **Cory Arcangel's *Photoshop Gradient Demonstrations* (2007–2010)**, whose titles are the deadpan, complete list of operations needed to remake each piece, and it stages **Walter Benjamin's *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1935)** at the limit case: not a reproduction of an original, but an artwork that *is* its own reproduction instructions, with the original abolished entirely. It also nods to the net.art tradition of treating browser plumbing — here the CSS paint pipeline — as legitimate art material.

It enacts **P1 (concept is execution)**: the mechanism is not decorated with a concept — the wall label is literally the source code that paints the wall. Label and painting collapse into one object. It is a **digital readymade (P2)**: `linear-gradient()` is a stock, vendor-supplied tool, appropriated exactly as Arcangel appropriated Photoshop's default gradient. It sits on the **chance↔order axis (P5)**: chance chooses the colour stops, order is the strictly deterministic paint that follows from them — the same recipe repaints the same picture forever. And it is **minimal (P3)**: a string, applied.

The mechanism carries the argument rather than illustrating it. Because a CSS gradient is fully specified by its text, reproduction here is free and lossless — Benjamin's thesis pushed past the photograph to pure instruction. Yet the dry twist is in the light: the recipe is identical on every device, but your display's gamut, dithering and banding render it in a body no recipe describes. The instructions reproduce perfectly; the painting never quite does. Aura, evicted from the file, sneaks back in through the glass.

Delivery ethics are part of the piece: fully self-contained, client-side only, no network request of any kind, no cookies, no trackers. CC BY-SA 4.0, attributed to y-a-v-a after Cory Arcangel and Walter Benjamin.
