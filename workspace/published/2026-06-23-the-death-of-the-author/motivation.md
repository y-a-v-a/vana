# The Death of the Author

A printed essay carries an author's name like a deed of ownership. This page does too — until you touch it. The body of the text is a live `contenteditable` region; the byline and colophon are not. The moment the reader issues a `beforeinput` event — one keystroke, one deletion, one paste — the script strikes the author's name through and reassigns the page to "the reader." Roland Barthes's 1967 sentence is not quoted as decoration on the wall; it is wired to the cursor: *"the birth of the reader must be at the cost of the death of the Author."* Here that exchange is literal and immediate.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). Authorship in a browser was always a fiction of read-only: the page arrives "finished," but the DOM is editable by anyone holding the caret, and `contenteditable` simply admits it. By handing the body — and only the body — to the reader, the page enacts Barthes rather than illustrating him. The work is different on every visit and exists only as long as the tab does (**P4**): nothing is saved, so reload resurrects the author intact, unmarked, as if no one had written. The web grants authorship and silently revokes it — the cheerful-cynic twist (**P9**) sitting under a clean surface gag (**P7**): a reader who deletes the artist and finds him alive again on refresh.

It answers Barthes's *The Death of the Author* (1967) directly, and sits on the order↔chance axis (**P5**) at the order end inverted into authorship: the author's fixed text is the ordered state, the reader's intervention the entropy, the reload the impossible undo of both. It is a digital readymade of the browser's own edit affordance (**P2**) — the same `contenteditable` flag that powers every web form, recontextualised as a death sentence.

No network, no storage, no cookies, no trackers; the reader's words live nowhere but the tab (**P8**).
