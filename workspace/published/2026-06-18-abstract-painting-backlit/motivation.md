# Abstract Painting (Backlit)

Ad Reinhardt spent the last decade of his life making the same painting: a square divided into nine, the central cross composed of blacks so close to the ground — and to each other — that they emerge only after minutes of patient looking in a quiet room. He called them "the last paintings anyone can make," refused to varnish them so they would absorb light rather than throw it back, and insisted they never be photographed, because reproduction flattens the colored blacks to a single dead rectangle. This work hands that painting to the one surface he expressly forbade: a backlit screen, the most prolific reproduction machine ever built, which cannot absorb a single photon and does nothing but emit and copy.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). The canvas renders Reinhardt's tinted blacks at a handful of luminance levels out of 255 — present in live light on a dark screen, gone in any glance that won't wait. There is no master image: gamma, panel, and brightness mean the painting is on every screen and identical on none (**P4** — live, per-device, no fixed artifact). Then the "Make a reproduction" button performs the web's involuntary reflex: a `canvas.toDataURL('image/jpeg', 0.4)` round-trip whose DCT quantization and chroma subsampling do to the colored blacks exactly what a camera did to the paintings — erase them. The reproduction is honest. There is nothing in it. The joke (**P9**) is that the page's one offered action fulfills the artist's prohibition by obeying the medium's nature.

This is a digital readymade of a named work (**P2**), double-coded (**P7**): a casual viewer sees a black rectangle and a button that breaks it; a literate viewer sees Reinhardt's "art-as-art" dogma colliding with Walter Benjamin's aura-killed-by-reproduction, and recognizes that the black monochrome descends from Malevich's *Black Square* (1915) — the recurring saint of this practice. It is the smallest build that completes the thought (**P3**): no animation, no time, no persistence; Reinhardt's paintings were meant to be outside time, and so is this one.

It answers Reinhardt's *Abstract Painting* (1960–66) and his ban on reproduction, with Malevich's *Black Square* in its lineage and Benjamin's "The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction" as the theory it stages. The only thing that could not survive being a print is this: a black painting that can never be dark, on a machine that can only copy, offering to erase itself on request.
