# A Humument (Select to Treat)

Tom Phillips spent sixty years *treating* a forgotten 1892 Victorian novel — W. H. Mallock's *A Human Document* — by painting, drawing and collaging over its pages until only a scatter of surviving words remained, linked into a new book he called *A Humument*. The gesture is erasure-as-authorship: you compose not by writing but by choosing what to leave. This work asks the browser to perform exactly that gesture with the single most ordinary thing a reader ever does online — selecting text.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). On the web, selecting words means: highlight, copy, own. Here the native highlight is deliberately suppressed (`::selection { background: transparent }`) and the `selectionchange` event does something else entirely — everything you did *not* select is painted over, receding into the page's ground, while the words you kept survive as ink. Selection stops being extraction and becomes treatment. You cannot do this to a printed page, and a print cannot heal itself the instant you let go; this could only live in a browser (**G1**). It is a digital readymade of Phillips's method (**P2**), built as the smallest thing that completes the thought — one span per word, one event handler, no libraries (**P3**).

It sits cleanly on the chance/order axis (**P5**): the source passage is fixed and ordered — a dense period pastiche about a connoisseur who cannot tell whether a picture has any value or whether the value is only "a rumour that has learned to hold still" (**P6** — the recurring subject of worth and authenticity). What the reader carves out of it is pure chance and choosing. Two readers make two poems; the same reader never makes the same one twice, because the ground hue is re-minted per visit and **nothing is stored** (no cookies, no network, no `localStorage`). Release the selection and the page heals — Phillips's treatment was permanent oil and ink; the web's is a breath (**P4, P8**).

It is double-coded (**P7**): a casual visitor gets a quiet reading toy — drag across words and a hidden poem surfaces. A literate visitor gets the whole argument about the death of the author and found poetry — that the reader is the artist, that composition is subtraction, and that on the web even authorship is unsaved. The wit (**P9**) is dry and structural: the browser's most acquisitive gesture is quietly redirected into an act of erasure, and the "expensive object in the room" is left for you to keep or paint over.

**Answers:** Tom Phillips, *A Humument* (1966– ); W. H. Mallock, *A Human Document* (1892); the erasure/found-poetry tradition; Marcel Duchamp / the readymade; net.art (the Selection API as material).
