# Equivalent (Any of the Fourteen)

In 1966 Carl Andre laid 120 firebricks on the floor in eight different rectangles and called them *Equivalent I–VIII*: same matter, same mass, eight shapes, none worth more than another. When the Tate showed *Equivalent VIII* the British press detonated — a national scandal about a "pile of bricks" the museum had paid for. The outrage was never about the bricks. It was about value: the suspicion that arrangement, and a museum's say-so, had conjured worth out of identical units of nothing.

Andre's eight were not arbitrary. His selection rule was plain — stack the bricks two courses high and take *every* rectangle whose shortest side is at least three bricks. For a footprint of sixty that rule resolves to exactly four shapes and their transposes: eight. The eight were never the point; they were what one not-very-divisible number happened to permit. This work keeps Andre's rule untouched and feeds it a number with more factors. A footprint of 180 — 360 bricks across two courses — admits fourteen equivalents instead of eight. Push to 5040 (seven factorial, divisible nearly every way a number can be) and the same rule opens dozens. The matter never changes; only the count of shapes it consents to.

On every click the page tears down its plan and re-lays the *same* 360 bricks into a different one of those fourteen rectangles. The count is hard-coded and invariant — the page cannot show you more bricks or fewer, only another proportion. A running line insists, dryly, that you are still looking at 360 bricks, still equivalent, however many times you reshuffle them. A faint "of XIV" beside each Roman numeral keeps the new tally honest: this is *one* of fourteen, where Andre had eight. The reshuffle is free; the matter is constant; the "edition" you happen to land on is pure chance.

It enacts **P1** (concept *is* execution): equivalence is not described, it is enforced — the only thing the mechanism can vary is shape, never quantity, so no rearrangement can be richer than the last. It sits squarely on the **P5** chance/order axis — each footprint is a strict rectangular order derived by factoring, but which of the fourteen you receive is a coin-toss — and it points straight at **P6**, value and authenticity, the Tate-bricks question "but is it art, and why does it cost that." It is **P3** minimal: a grid of identical divs, a divisor loop, and a number that never changes. And it double-codes (**P7**): a casual viewer gets a tidy floor of bricks that keep rearranging themselves; a literate one gets *Equivalent VIII*, the whole 1976 tabloid argument about money and museums, and the quiet maths joke that Andre's "eight" was only ever a property of the number sixty.

The reference it answers is Carl Andre, *Equivalent VIII* (1966) and the Tate acquisition of 1972 — and behind it the readymade's original provocation, that designation, not labour, makes the thing art. The refinement also answers the original *Equivalent (Any of the Eight)*: by computing the family from Andre's own rule rather than hard-listing it, the work shows that the size of the edition is a function of arithmetic, not of the artist's whim. The mechanism is the argument because the web is the perfect medium for *equivalence*: a per-click re-render costs nothing, persists nothing, and stores nothing, so it demonstrates in real time what scandalised London — that the arrangement is variable and weightless while the value claim stays stubbornly fixed. As a print this dies: a photograph would freeze one of the fourteen and quietly make it *the* one, which is exactly the lie the piece refuses. CC BY-SA, no cookies, no tracking, nothing kept.
