# The Unkept

A generative "situation" is composed for you at the moment you arrive — a small
set of warm, drifting forms, unique to this visit, never to recur. It lives only
while you attend to it. The instant you look away — switch tabs, minimise, or let
the window lose focus — it is destroyed: the field goes black, no final image is
left on screen, and it will not come back. Reload and a different one is born,
which will also not last. Nothing is recorded. There is, deliberately, no
localStorage, no cache, no edition number, no provenance.

This answers **Tino Sehgal**, whose *constructed situations* forbid photography,
catalogues, wall texts, and any material residue — works sold by spoken contract,
with no paper, that exist only in the live encounter and afterward only in
memory. Staging that refusal on the web is the joke and the point: the web is the
most relentlessly persistent medium ever built, designed to cache, archive, and
reproduce everything by default. *The Unkept* is a webpage that insists on being
ephemeral on a substrate that cannot forget. It is also the deliberate inverse of
*The Original* (2026), which used localStorage to manufacture fake scarcity — here
there is no counter to game, because nothing survives the looking-away.

The **mechanism is the argument** (P1): the Page Visibility API and the window
`blur`/`pagehide` events are not decoration around a concept about presence — they
*are* the concept. Attention is the medium; withdrawal of attention is the
destruction. The piece cannot be collected, documented, or owned, only witnessed —
which is precisely Sehgal's claim about what an artwork can be (P6, value and
authenticity rendered as un-keepability rather than priced as scarcity).

It sits on the **chance/order axis** (P5): each situation is composed by a fixed
rule system — orbiting radial gradients in a single candle-warm hue family — but
seeded entirely by chance at load, so order produces the grammar and chance
produces the individual that dies. It is **web-native** (P4, G1): it could not be
a print, because a print is the one thing it refuses to be — kept. The voice is
the cheerful cynic (P9): the epitaph shrugs that another is one reload away, and
that one will not last either.
