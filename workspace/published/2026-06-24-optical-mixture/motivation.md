# Optical Mixture

A square of cream "canvas" carries a field of small complementary dots — orange and blue. Held at a normal distance and a normal zoom, the dots are too small and too adjacent to be read separately, so the retina averages them: they resolve into a luminous neutral disc, a soft light hovering over the ground. This is *optical mixture*, the discovery Georges Seurat built a method on — color combined not on the palette but in the eye of a viewer standing at the right distance. Divisionism is, quietly, a contract: the painting agrees to cohere only if you agree to keep your distance.

The web breaks that contract. Every browser hands the viewer infinite, free zoom, and a painting on a screen has no fixed viewing distance to assume. This work reads the viewer's zoom live (`window.visualViewport.scale` for pinch, `devicePixelRatio` relative to its load-time baseline for browser zoom) and, the moment you lean in, drives the dots apart — the closer you look, the further they drift, until the luminous disc you came for stops existing and only confetti remains. The wall label tracks it in real time: *viewing distance — optical mixture: active / VOID.*

This enacts **P1** — the concept and the mechanism are one object. The argument ("Pointillism is a promise about distance, and on the web distance is the viewer's to revoke") is not illustrated by the code; it *is* the code, because the zoom event literally voids the mix. It sits on the order side of **P5** (a strict deterministic dot grid, identical computation in every browser, with a hashed density falloff for the broken-color ground), points the **narrative** at perception and the silent conditions a medium assumes, and is double-coded per **P7**: a casual viewer gets the shimmer-then-shatter gag; a literate one gets Seurat, Signac, and Chevreul's law of simultaneous contrast. The voice (**P9**) is the dry cheerful cynic — the museum tells you to step close and admire the brushwork; here stepping close is exactly what destroys the painting.

It answers Georges Seurat's *Un dimanche après-midi à l'Île de la Grande Jatte* (1884–86) and the divisionist tradition, and it could not be a print: a print has one fixed dot size and one viewing distance baked in forever. This thing only exists in the negotiation between the screen, the eye, and a zoom control — a place only the browser provides.
