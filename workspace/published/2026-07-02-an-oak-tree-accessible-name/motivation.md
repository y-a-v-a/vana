# An Oak Tree (Accessible Name)

In 1973 Michael Craig-Martin put an ordinary glass of water on a glass shelf and, in an accompanying interview, declared that he had changed it into a full-grown oak tree — not a symbol of one, an actual oak tree — without altering its appearance. The work is not the glass. The work is the *declaration*, and your willingness to take the artist at his word. It is the purest statement of the conceptual thesis running from Duchamp's readymade through Danto's "artworld": what a thing *is* as art is assigned by designation, not by how it looks.

The web already ships this piece in every image tag. An `<img>` has pixels — what you see — and it has an `alt` / accessible name — the authoritative text declaring *what the picture is*, the caption a screen reader announces and a search engine records when it cannot see. Appearance and declared identity are structurally separate fields. So here the picture is a plain glass of water, and one button — "Consecrate" — changes only the accessible name from «A glass of water» to «An oak tree». The readout is read live from the DOM, so it reports what the browser would genuinely say, and the code snapshots the SVG before and after to attest that *not one pixel moved*. Transubstantiation with zero visible change: exactly Craig-Martin's claim, now performed by HTML's own accessibility contract.

This enacts **P1** (the mechanism *is* the argument: the `alt`/accessible-name field literally is Craig-Martin's declaration, so consecrating the image and captioning it are one act, not two), **P2** (the digital readymade — HTML's separation of pixels from declared identity is found, not built), **P3** (one button, one attribute, nothing else), and **P7** (surface gag: the caption says oak tree, you see water; underneath: the entire designation-theory of art). It sits firmly on the **order** end of P5 — this is language and rule, the Kosuth/Weiner/Duchamp declarative lineage — not chance.

For **narrative**, it answers the catalogue's own Magritte, *la-trahison-des-images.be* ("this is not a pipe"). Magritte and Kosuth's *One and Three Chairs* stage the *gap* between word and thing — semiotic doubt, denial. Craig-Martin closes the gap by faith: "it is not a symbol, it is an oak tree" — affirmation by declaration. This is the affirmative twin of the responsive pipe. Where la-trahison uses layout to prove a picture is *not* its object, this uses the accessible name to insist the picture *is* something it visibly is not — and asks you to believe it.

The delivery keeps the ethic: fully self-contained, inline SVG, no external requests, no cookies, no storage. Consecration is irreversible within a visit (an act of faith cannot be quietly taken back); reload restores the water, because the web forgets.
