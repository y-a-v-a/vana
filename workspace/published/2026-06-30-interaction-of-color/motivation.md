# Interaction of Color

Two squares sit on two grounds. They are, to the byte, one and the same colour — and almost nobody sees them that way. Josef Albers built his whole pedagogy (*Interaction of Color*, 1963; the *Homage to the Square* series) on this single scandal: "colour is the most relative medium in art." A colour has no fixed identity; it is only ever what its neighbours let it be. The page stages exactly that, with grounds chosen fresh on every visit so the work has no canonical appearance — the same chip reads dark-and-warm on the pale ground, light-and-cool on the dark one.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). The work makes two opposite claims at once and lets the medium carry both: the **display** performs difference (simultaneous contrast — your retina lying in good faith), while `getComputedStyle` performs identity, printing the two chips' RGB values side by side and the verdict `identical → true`. The browser is at once the liar and the lie-detector. Crucially, the proof is a *motion only the live medium can make*: press the button and the two squares slide off their grounds onto a neutral grey judge, abut, and merge into one seamless rectangle. Paper cannot slide a square out of its context on demand; this is why the piece would die as a print (**G1**). It is a **digital readymade** (**P2**) — the readymade here is the engine's own colour pipeline — kept to the smallest build that completes the thought (**P3**).

It sits on the **order** end of the chance/order axis (**P5**): deterministic HSL arithmetic generates chip and grounds, with only the seed left to chance, so the rule stays legible. And it is double-coded (**P7**): a casual visitor gets a clean optical gag — "wait, those *are* the same?" — while the literate viewer gets the Albers argument about the non-existence of stable colour, and behind it the older lineage of simultaneous contrast. The voice is the dry cheerful-cynic (**P9, G5**): the wall text swears the squares are identical and adds, in the data plate, `your eye → "no"`.

It answers Albers directly, and takes its place in the catalogue's perception thread (Seurat's *optical mixture*, Reinhardt's near-threshold black) without repeating it: optical mixture fuses *separate* marks at distance; this is the opposite trick — one *single* colour split into two by context alone.
