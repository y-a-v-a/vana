# Acid Action Painting (Reload to Resurrect)

In 1960, on London's South Bank, Gustav Metzger sprayed hydrochloric acid onto sheets of nylon stretched on a frame. The sheets dissolved in seconds, holes opening and spreading until nothing was left. He called it *auto-destructive art*: a work whose subject is its own predetermined death, made (per his 1959 manifesto) to "self-destruct within a time period not exceeding twenty years." It was an anti-monument and an anti-commodity — a painting you could neither keep nor resell.

This page is one nylon sheet. A rectangular field of cells (order) is eaten by a chance-shuffled wave of dissolution (P5) over a declared, finite lifespan of thirty seconds. The destruction is not animated decoration: the cells are real DOM nodes and the acid genuinely removes them. When the field is empty the page does not stop at the picture — it removes *every* element under the `<body>`: the title, the wall label, the status, and the script's own node last of all. The body is left genuinely empty, a real deleted document, with the means of destruction destroying itself last. Concept *is* execution (P1): the page does not depict auto-destruction, it performs it on its own body until there is no body left to look at. It is the smallest build that completes the thought (P3) and a fresh per-visit behaviour rather than a fixed artifact (P4).

The twist is the medium's confession (P7, P9). Metzger's art could die exactly once; that mortality *was* the work. The web, by contrast, is the machine of the perfect copy. Here the document erases itself to nothing — and the single trace that survives is *not* on the page at all: it is the `<title>` tag in the head, which flips to **"(deleted — reload to resurrect)."** Pressing reload restores the painting flawlessly, which the original acid action could never do. On the web nothing truly dies; it can *perform* death but never *suffer* it. This answers Metzger directly and, underneath, Walter Benjamin's *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1935): reproduction abolishes the aura by abolishing finality. The page can stage auto-destruction and is constitutionally incapable of achieving it — a cheerful-cynic short circuit between the most destructible art of the century and the most resurrectable medium of ours.

The refinement pushes the destruction past the painting and into the page itself: nothing in the body is permitted to outlive the work, not even the wall label that, in a museum, always survives the picture it describes. Documentation does not vanish, though — it *migrates*. It moves out of the DOM body and into the browser's own chrome, the tab title, the last place a label can outlive a body. An empty deleted document whose only caption lives in the browser frame is a more honestly web-native ending than any surviving on-page text. References answered: Gustav Metzger, *Acid Action Painting* (1960) and the *Auto-Destructive Art* manifesto (1959); Walter Benjamin (1935); and, as a sibling self-destroying machine, Jean Tinguely's *Homage to New York* (1960).
