# Pentimento (Nothing Painted Over)

A *pentimento* — Italian for "repentance" — is a painter's change of mind that was meant to disappear: an arm repositioned, a face painted out, a whole figure buried under the finished surface. You are only supposed to see it centuries later, when the top layer thins with age or an X-ray looks underneath. Oil hides its second thoughts; the correction is a secret the picture keeps from you. This work stages the web's flat refusal of that secret. HTML has two semantic elements for revision — `<del>` and `<ins>` — and the browser's *own* user-agent stylesheet, with no CSS asked of it, strikes the deletion through and keeps the insertion, side by side, permanently, on the surface. The repentance is worn on the outside. The struck words are never painted over; they are struck through and left legible.

The mechanism *is* the argument (P1). The whole piece is a short artist's statement that deflates itself in front of you — *masterpiece* → *picture*, *the Louvre* → *your screen* — and every downgrade is real `<del>`/`<ins>` markup, not styling. Read it with the strikes and you get the confession; hit **Show the finished picture** and a single class sets `del { display:none }`, and the clean, "finished" statement surfaces from the *identical, unchanged source* — no fetch, no overwrite, both readings resident in one document at once (P3: the user-agent stylesheet does the labour, the build is almost nothing). That is the thing paint physically cannot do: hold the buried version and the final version in the same object, switchable, with nothing destroyed.

The second proof is reproduction. Because a struck-through `<del>` is still real text, it lives in `textContent` — so it lives in the clipboard. **Copy the statement** and paste it: the deletions come with you. The ledger names this directly (*what your eye reads / what the clipboard carries*): the surface never held the whole truth, and every copy smuggles the correction out. Where Walter Benjamin's mechanical reproduction strips the aura, here reproduction does the opposite of forgetting — it carries the pentimento the original tried to hide. X-ray, for free (P4, P7).

It answers a specific work by inverting it. Rauschenberg's *Erased de Kooning Drawing* (1953) makes art by *removing* — a drawing rubbed to near-nothing, the erasure irreversible and total (the catalogue's own *Erased (No Undo)* honours that destruction). This is the mirror: nothing is erased. The count of words *actually discarded* is fixed at **0**. Every repentance stays in the source, indexable, selectable, permanent — the browser will not let a correction die. Lillian Hellman's memoir *Pentimento* (1973) gave the term its popular gloss: "the old conception, replaced by a later choice, is a way of seeing and then seeing again." The web sees, and then sees again, and keeps both.

**DNA principles:** P1 (concept is execution — the `<del>`/`<ins>` markup and its default rendering *are* the pentimento), P2 (digital readymade — appropriating the user-agent stylesheet's built-in revision rendering), P3 (minimal gesture — one class toggle over browser defaults), P4 (live — both states and the deleted matter persist as document state, and travel through the clipboard), P7 (double-coding — surface: a crossed-out self-deflating statement; depth: the web's exact inversion of pentimento and of erasure).
