# The Order of Things

Nine artists sit in a list, filed alphabetically. Then you press *Svenska* and Öyvind Fahlström — comfortably mid-pack a second ago — drops to the bottom of the drawer, because in Swedish, Ö sorts after Z. Press *Español (trad.)* and Cornell slips ahead of Chillida, because traditional Spanish treats *ch* as its own letter after *c*. The list never changes; only its order does, and the order is not ours to keep. "Alphabetical" turns out to be a question — alphabetical *according to whom?* — answered not by the artist but by whichever collation table the reader's browser happens to ship.

The mechanism is the argument (**P1**, **G3**). The precedence is computed entirely by the browser's built-in `Intl.Collator`, whose locale tables are the same ICU machinery a bank uses to sort your name. Nothing is decorative: the ranking you see *is* a live query to a cultural rulebook baked into your device. On load the page opens the drawer in *your* locale (`navigator.language`), so the ideology arrives addressed to you — a small Haacke-style implication of the viewer in the system being critiqued. This is a **digital readymade** (**P2**): the un-glamorous i18n plumbing every web app leans on, lifted onto the wall and asked to confess that it has an opinion. It could not survive as a print — a print freezes one order and calls it neutral; only the running collator can show the order dissolving under your hands (**P4**, **G1**).

It answers Alighiero Boetti's *Classifying the thousand longest rivers in the world* (1977), a work whose whole substance is the arbitrary labour of ranking, and it sits squarely on Boetti's own *ordine e disordine* axis (**P5**, order pole — but an order revealed as contingent). Underneath runs Borges' "Analytical Language of John Wilkins" and Foucault's *Les mots et les choses*: the discovery that every taxonomy smuggles in a worldview. It also answers y-a-v-a's own *Modernipsum* — where art discourse was the readymade, here the archive's *filing system* is.

The double coding (**P7**): the casual visitor gets a clean toy — click a language, watch the names race past each other — while the literate visitor reads the institutional-critique argument that classification is never neutral and the "universal" alphabet is a local custom wearing a lab coat. The tone stays dry and cheerful-cynic (**P9**): no outrage, just a caption that shrugs, *"Same list. Their order is not."* The build is the smallest that completes the thought (**P3**) — nine names, one `Intl.Collator`, a FLIP animation so precedence is visibly *taken* rather than merely stated — served under CC BY-SA with no fonts, requests, storage, or trackers (**P8**, **G4**).
