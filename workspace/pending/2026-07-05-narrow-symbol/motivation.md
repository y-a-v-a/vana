# Narrow Symbol

The painting is a single, huge currency symbol, drawn in whatever cursive hand the device happens to own — Andy Warhol's *Dollar Sign* (1981), but the sign is not necessarily a dollar. It is the symbol your browser assigns you. An American arrives to `$`, a Briton to `£`, a Japanese visitor to `¥`, a Russian to `₽`. Beneath it, a wall label prices the work at a fixed magnitude — **1,000,000** — formatted through `Intl.NumberFormat({style:'currency'})`. Press *Revalue* and the same number, unchanged, becomes a fortune, then a modest sum, then almost nothing, as the tour moves from USD to JPY to NGN. The digits never move. Only the glyph does.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). The price is not depicted; it is *computed by the browser's internationalization data* — the same locale tables that decide how your bank statement reads. Because the page makes no network request of any kind, there is no exchange rate present: nothing can convert one of these prices into another, or check any of them against a real bank. The incommensurability is not a claim in a caption; it is a structural fact of an offline document. The browser presents `¥1,000,000` and `$1,000,000` with exactly equal authority and no way to reconcile them — value revealed as pure typography (**P6**, **P7**). This could not survive as a print: a print fixes one currency, and Warhol's most American icon would stay American. Here the browser re-nationalizes it per visitor (**G1**).

It is a digital readymade (**P2**): `Intl.NumberFormat`, a plumbing API meant to make software polite across borders, is appropriated as a value-machine. The smallest build completes the thought (**P3**) — one symbol, one label, one button. The tour deliberately includes `ru-RU`, a wink to Alexander Brener spraying a dollar sign onto a Malevich in 1997, threading this into y-a-v-a's own *Art is Money* (2013) without repeating it.

The named answers are **Andy Warhol, *Dollar Sign* (1981)** for the image and **J.S.G. Boggs** — who hand-drew banknotes and spent them at face value — for the argument that a currency's worth is a shared fiction and its symbol does all the work. The viewer's own currency is *guessed* from their language and honestly flagged as an assumption when the language names no country: the browser doesn't actually know your money, it infers it, one more soft fiction stacked on the hard one. Dry, not preachy (**P9**): same number, different fortune, and no teller in the building to settle which is true. Delivered CC BY-SA, no cookies, no tracking, no network (**P8**).
