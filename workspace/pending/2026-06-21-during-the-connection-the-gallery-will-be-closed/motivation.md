# During the Connection the Gallery Will Be Closed

In 1969 Robert Barry handed the Amsterdam gallery Art & Project a sentence as an exhibition: *During the exhibition the gallery will be closed.* The institution shut its doors for the run of the show; the closure was the work. This piece inverts the proposition for the network age. Here the "gallery" — the artwork — is closed for exactly as long as you remain connected to the internet. To enter, you must leave the web that delivered you: switch on airplane mode, unplug, drop the Wi-Fi. The instant the browser believes you are offline, the page turns from charcoal "CLOSED" to gallery-white "OPEN," and a small clock counts the private, unrecorded duration of your visit. Reconnect and the door swings shut.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). The work reads `navigator.onLine` and listens for the browser's `online`/`offline` events; that single binary — the browser's own belief about whether it can reach the world — is the gallery door. No content is fetched to test the connection and nothing is sent (G1, **P8**); the page only asks "am I online?" and trusts the answer, which is itself the point: connectivity is treated as a self-reported institutional state, openable and closeable like a physical door. This is a **digital readymade** (**P2**) — appropriating a mundane platform signal (the offline event, normally used to scold you with "you are offline") and recontextualising it as an admission ticket.

Double-coded as the DNA requires (**P7**): the casual visitor gets an immediate, dry gag — *turn off your internet to see the internet art* — and the literate visitor gets the lineage to Barry's closed gallery and to net.art's central embarrassment, that the most anti-institutional medium is wholly dependent on the infrastructure it pretends to escape. What you find on the other side of disconnection is, deliberately, an empty open room (cheerful cynic, **P9**): the network promised so much, and the work you sacrificed your bandwidth to reach is your own solitude, timed and immediately forgotten.

It sits on the presence/absence pole of the practice rather than chance/order, and answers Barry directly while echoing Lee Lozano's *General Strike Piece* — withdrawal as the gesture. Minimal build, smallest gesture that completes the thought (**P3**, **P4**): a few CSS state rules, twenty lines of vanilla JS, one event pair, no storage, no canvas, no dependency. The exhibition that can only open when the medium fails.
