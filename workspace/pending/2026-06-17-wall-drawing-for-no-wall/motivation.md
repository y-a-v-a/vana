# Wall Drawing for No Wall

The artwork here is not the picture. It is the **instruction** printed beneath it — "One hundred straight lines… each connecting two points chosen at random on the edges of the square… the draftsman decides where each line falls." The drawing above is merely *one execution* of that sentence. Reload the page, or summon another draftsman, and the same instruction yields a different picture: equally faithful, equally valid, equally finished. This is exactly how Sol LeWitt's wall drawings work — the certificate of a few sentences is the owned, authored, salable object; the painted wall is anonymous, re-executable by any hand, and destroyed when the exhibition closes.

This piece answers **Sol LeWitt's wall drawings** and his line from *Paragraphs on Conceptual Art* (1967): "The idea becomes a machine that makes the art." The web takes him at his word. The browser is the machine; client-side JavaScript is the anonymous draftsman; your screen is the borrowed wall, repainted on every visit and erased the moment you navigate away. The instruction deliberately under-determines the result, so chance lives *inside* the order — which places the work squarely on the **chance↔order axis**, but argues a different point than rule-based geometry pieces do: not "system versus entropy," but **authorship versus execution**, the conceptual-art separation that LeWitt invented.

It enacts **P1** literally: the instruction text and the rendered lines are generated from the *same* parameters in source (the counts, the four colors), so concept and execution are provably one object rather than an idea decorated with code. It is a **digital readymade (P2)** of LeWitt's instruction form, **live and regenerating per visit (P4)**, and turns on **value and authenticity (P6)** — a LeWitt certificate has sold for over a million dollars precisely *because* the wall is worthless and repaintable. Here the same kind of object is given away under **CC BY-SA (P8)**, executed for free, forever, by a draftsman who will never be paid and never be named.

The mechanism is the argument. A static print could show you *a* LeWitt-like drawing, but it could never show you that the drawing is interchangeable — that the artwork survives its own erasure and re-execution, that the idea outlives every instance. Only a thing that re-draws itself, differently and on demand, in front of you, can make that case. The web does for nothing what the market sells as scarcity, and it does it by being honest about what the artwork always was: a set of instructions, and a wall it does not own. **(P3, P5, P7, P9.)**
