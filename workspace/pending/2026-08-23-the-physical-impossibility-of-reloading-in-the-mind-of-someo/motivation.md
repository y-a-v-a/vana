# The Physical Impossibility of Reloading in the Mind of Someone Browsing

Damien Hirst suspended a tiger shark in formaldehyde to make death sit still and look alive, then watched the market pay eight figures for it — and later had to quietly replace the original when it rotted, gutting its own authenticity. This work does the same trick with the one thing the browser already keeps in suspended animation: the back/forward cache. When you leave a page, a modern browser does not kill it. It *freezes* it — JavaScript halted, the render loop paused, the entire live document pickled. Press Back and it revives exactly where it was, mid-motion, the very same fish. The bfcache is 5% formaldehyde.

The **mechanism is the argument** (P1). Nothing here illustrates preservation; the preservation is real and native. Switch tabs or navigate away and the `requestAnimationFrame` loop genuinely stops — the shark hangs, suspended, because the browser refuses to spend a cycle painting what you are not looking at. Return, and the `pageshow` event fires with `persisted === true`, the Page Lifecycle `resume` event confirms the revival, and the frame gap measures precisely how long the specimen was in the tank. A reload takes the other path — `persisted === false` — and resets the catch: a *new* specimen, "the previous shark having decomposed," which is exactly the 2006 replacement scandal rendered as a button (P6, value and authenticity as targets).

It is a **digital readymade** (P2): the bfcache, `freeze`/`resume`, and `pageshow.persisted` are appropriated web plumbing, recontextualised as a formaldehyde vitrine complete with the institutional apparatus Hirst's market demands — steel-framed tank, wall label, medium line, price realised, red SOLD dot. The reference it answers is *The Physical Impossibility of Death in the Mind of Someone Living* (1991); the title is a direct détournement (P9, humour as carrier). The double coding (P7): a casual viewer gets a shark in a tank and a "do not reload or you kill it" gag; the literate viewer gets an argument about aura, preservation, and the replaced-original authenticity crisis, staged on the browser's own suspension model.

It sits toward the **order** end of the chance/order axis (P5) — a deterministic behaviour, not a random composition — and it is unambiguously **live, not fixed** (P4): the work only exists while the tab is open, and only persists across a departure while the browser chooses to keep it cached. There is no storage, no cookie, no network request of any kind (P8); the shark cannot be saved, only kept alive by attention or pickled by the cache. When a browser declines to bfcache the page, the return is a fresh catch instead of a revival — the refusal becomes part of the piece, honestly reported in the live readout rather than hidden.

**References answered:** Damien Hirst, *The Physical Impossibility of Death in the Mind of Someone Living* (1991) and its 2006 replaced-shark authenticity scandal; Walter Benjamin on aura and reproduction. **Principles enacted:** P1, P2, P4, P5 (order), P6, P7, P8, P9.
