# Le Grand Verre (Nothing of Its Own)

Duchamp did not call *The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even* a painting. He called it "a delay in glass" — a transparent support with no image of its own, whose picture is completed by whatever room stands behind it. This work rebuilds that ontology out of a single CSS property. The two panes here have `background: transparent` and `background-image: none` (the page attests both live, via `getComputedStyle`); every pixel you see *inside* them is a `backdrop-filter` — a blurred, sepia'd pass-through of the apparatus painted behind. The glass owns nothing. It borrows.

The proof is the button. **Remove the room behind the glass** and the `backdrop-filter` has nothing left to filter: the panes go flat and empty, disclosing that they never held a picture — only a view. This is the mechanism *being* the argument (**P1**), not illustrating it. You cannot separate "the idea that the Large Glass is defined by what is behind it" from the code, because the code is a compositing operation that literally renders only what is behind it. It could not survive as a print: a print is opaque and owns its ink; a delay in glass owns nothing and dies when the room is switched off. That is the web-native, only-here gesture (**G1**).

It answers a specific work — Duchamp's *Large Glass* (1915–23) — and its most Duchampian fact: the panes shattered in transit in 1927, and he declared the cracks a completion "by chance," keeping them. So the cracks here are re-thrown on every visit from `crypto.getRandomValues` — canned chance radiating from a random impact point (**P5**, the chance pole; **P2**, the digital readymade of a canonical object). The order pole is the schematic apparatus behind the glass: the nine malic moulds, the chocolate grinder, the Bride's garment horizon, drawn as strict line-work. Chance sits on order, exactly as Duchamp staged it.

Read at the surface it is a small glass gag: a cracked pane you can wipe the room out from behind. Read with the reference it is an argument about where an artwork lives — in the object, or in the transaction between object and viewer's room (**P7**). The build is the smallest that completes the thought (**P3**): one CSS property, one toggle, one attestation line; no library, no network, no storage.
