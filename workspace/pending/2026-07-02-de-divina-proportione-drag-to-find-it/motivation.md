# De Divina Proportione (Drag to Find It)

Art history loves to whisper that its masterpieces secretly obey φ, the golden ratio — the Parthenon, Leonardo, Mondrian, Le Corbusier's Modulor. Luca Pacioli literally called it *De divina proportione* (1509) and had Leonardo draw the plates. This page hands you the sculptor's tool and dares you to arrive at the divine. The rectangle carries the browser's native `resize: both` grip in its lower-right corner. Grab it, stretch, and a live plate reports your ratio to six decimals against φ = 1.618034… The verdict is always the same: **not divine**, and the exact distance you fell short.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). The whirling-squares subdivision drawn on the canvas only nests cleanly, level after level, at φ; the instant your hand-dragged rectangle drifts off it, the construction degenerates after two or three cuts — so the picture's own depth is a ruler for how badly you missed. Meanwhile the numeric plate quantizes to whole pixels, and φ is irrational: the target sits in a gap the pixel grid cannot address. That gap is Duchamp's **infrathin** — the immeasurably small difference between "almost" and "exactly" — rendered as the space between your ratio and the divine one. Should the counter ever flip to DIVINE, it labels itself honestly: *reached by accident*. Chance, not genius, and never authority (**P5**, chance versus the ordered ideal).

The readymade here is the corner-drag handle itself (**P2**): a scrap of browser chrome most sites suppress, promoted to the chisel that shapes the canvas. The build is the smallest that completes the thought (**P3**) — one resizable box, one canvas, one plate, no libraries, no network. It reads at two depths (**P7**): a casual visitor plays a "find the sweet spot" game and keeps losing; a literate one watches the golden-ratio mysticism that decorates a century of art criticism get quietly refuted by a `ResizeObserver`. The tone is the cheerful cynic (**P9**): the divine is offered, and withheld, by a widget.

It answers Pacioli and Leonardo's *De divina proportione* directly, and stands against the whole "the great works are secretly φ" folklore (the Modulor, the Parthenon legend) — the market's and the academy's favorite way to launder taste as mathematics (**P6**, value and authenticity). No print could do this: the work has no fixed dimensions until you give it some, and its only truth — that you cannot land the divine by hand — exists only in the live act of dragging.
