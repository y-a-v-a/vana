# The Difference — after Sturtevant

**Concept.** Elaine Sturtevant repainted other artists' works — Warhol's *Flowers*, Johns' flags, Lichtenstein's benday — so exactly that the only thing her art was *about* was the residue between an original and its copy. This piece stacks two identical renderings of the same Pop flower and composites the top one onto the bottom in the canvas `globalCompositeOperation = 'difference'` mode. Difference-blend subtracts one image from the other channel by channel: where the copy registers perfectly on its twin, the result is zero — pure black. A flawless reproduction is therefore *invisible*. The only art you can ever see is the misalignment you introduce by dragging: every visible petal is a registration error, and the live readout reports it as "visible art %."

**Principles enacted.** **P1 (concept *is* execution):** the difference operator is not a metaphor for Sturtevant's question, it literally *is* the subtraction — the machine computes "where is the difference?" and answers "nowhere" by going black. **P2 (digital readymade):** a canonical motif (Warhol's *Flowers*) is appropriated exactly as Sturtevant appropriated it, then re-appropriated through a browser blend mode. **P6 (value / authenticity):** fidelity and visibility are inverse — perfect authenticity of the copy yields zero to look at; the "artwork" lives entirely in the flaw. **P5 (order↔chance):** the render is strictly deterministic; the only entropy is the viewer's hand on the pointer. **P3, P7, P9:** smallest build that completes the thought; a spot-the-difference gag on the surface, a Benjaminian argument about aura and reproduction underneath.

**Reference it answers.** Elaine Sturtevant, *Warhol Flowers* (1964–65), by way of Andy Warhol, *Flowers* (1964); and Walter Benjamin, *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1935). Within the y-a-v-a catalogue it answers **The Original (2026)** — where scarcity was exposed as fiction — by testing the *other* side of reproduction: not the edition count but the copy's likeness. When the copy is exact, there is no aura and nothing on screen.

**Why the mechanism is the argument, not decoration.** Nothing here illustrates a point with a picture. The difference compositing operation performs Benjamin's thesis in hardware: reproduction that is perfect leaves no trace, so the black canvas is the honest output of a faithful copy. You cannot make a print of this — a print cannot cancel itself to black and re-appear only where your hand slips. The verdict flips between "Nothing to see. The copy is perfect." and "You are looking at your own mistake." depending on a real pixel measurement, so the machine, not a wall text, delivers the punchline.
