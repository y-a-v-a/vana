# Hammer Price

A painting hangs with its auction label. Below it is a single bidding paddle — an `<input type="range">` — and the instruction *Your bid*. You raise it. The number climbs in your own browser's currency. Somewhere above sits a reserve you cannot see, drawn fresh on every visit. The moment your bid crosses it, the hammer falls: the canvas drains to grey, a veil reads *"Image withheld at the owner's request — withdrawn to a private collection,"* and the ledger settles. **Hammer price**, **consignor's profit**, and then the line that does the work: *The artist receives — $0.*

The mechanism *is* the argument (P1, P6). A range slider is, by definition, a value you push only upward; that is also the definition of speculative bidding. The act of valuing the work — wanting it, raising your paddle — is the exact act that pushes it past the reserve and out of public view. You do not acquire the painting by bidding; you disappear it into a vault. The control's literal operation enacts the market's: the more the public esteems a work, the faster it vanishes into private hands, and none of the new value ever flows back to the maker. This could not be a print — a print fixes one price; here the reserve is live, secret, per-visit chance against your monotonic order (P5), and the withdrawal happens in front of you.

It answers a specific event: the sale of the **Robert & Ethel Scull collection** at Sotheby Parke-Bernet, New York, on **18 October 1973** — the auction widely treated as the birth of the modern art market as speculation. A **Robert Rauschenberg** painting the Sculls had bought for $900 hammered at $85,000; Rauschenberg, present, shoved Scull and told him he'd been working his ass off so that *Scull* could make the profit. The work hard-codes his $900 floor and computes the consignor's gain live, so the resale windfall and the artist's nothing sit in the same ledger you just filled in. It extends the catalogue's value/authenticity line (*Three Ball Total Equilibrium Slot Machine*, *For the Love of Fame*, *Art is Money*) with a mechanism none of them used: the bid itself as the instrument of removal.

The voice stays dry rather than indignant (P9): no manifesto, just an auctioneer's deadpan — *"Reserve not yet met. Raise your paddle."* — and a balance sheet. The surface gag (you can "buy" a painting and it instantly leaves the room) lands without a wall text; the literate viewer reads the Scull sale and the resale-royalty argument underneath (P7). Open-licensed, client-side only, no storage and no network: the reserve is genuinely forgotten on reload, so nothing about this transaction is more real than the market it describes (P8).
