# Artist's Statement

A single field, labelled *Artist's Statement*, invites you to write. Click it and the browser's own autocomplete drops down the whole finite, scrollable list of sanctioned art-world terms — *spatiotemporal, liminal, praxis, the everyday, dematerialisation, the readymade.* You do not compose the statement; you select it. As you type, a live tally reports how many of your words the institution recognises, ending in the verdict *statement approved* or *not yet art.* Nothing is stored, nothing is sent.

The mechanism **is** the argument (P1). The concept — that originality in art discourse is the recombination of a closed, pre-approved vocabulary — is enacted by the native `<datalist>` element, whose defining property is exactly that: a *closed set* rendered as un-restylable OS chrome. Autocomplete's affordance (suggest-then-select) literally performs "the author chooses from what already exists." No text is generated and no randomness is needed; the point is that the words are already there, waiting, and writing collapses into picking from a menu the art world has vetted. This is the readymade turned on language itself (P2): the press-release lexicon appropriated and handed back for approval.

It answers a named web-native reference — Alix Rule & David Levine's essay **"International Art English"** (*Triple Canopy*, 2012), which diagnosed the strange dialect of the gallery press release — and it answers y-a-v-a's own **Modernipsum** (2012): where that work *generated* the discourse as lorem ipsum, this one merely offers the discourse back and grades your fluency in it. Behind both stands Duchamp's readymade and, in the lineage, the death of the author dissolved into a form control.

It is double-coded (P7): the casual viewer gets the immediate gag — click an empty statement box and the browser cheerfully offers to write your grand claims for you — while the literate viewer reads the argument about institutional language, gatekeeping, and authorship-as-selection. The build is the smallest that completes the thought (P3): one input, one datalist, a tally. And the anti-surveillance ethic is itself part of the piece (P8) — the confessional "statement" field that keeps no record and reaches no server, dry and cheerful about the fact that the machine will still tell you whether you passed.
