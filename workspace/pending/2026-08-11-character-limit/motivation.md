# Character Limit

**Concept.** The character counter is one of the web's most quietly powerful readymades: the "160" of SMS, the "280" of the tweet, the number that ticks toward red and decides whether you are permitted to speak. It looks like a neutral instrument. It is not. This page hands you that exact apparatus — a compose field and a live limit — and lets you discover, by typing, that the counter cannot agree with your own eye about what a single character even is. You type one family emoji. Your eye reads one thing. The counter charges you eleven and shrinks your allowance accordingly. Non-Latin scripts and emoji cost more room for the same visible utterance; the "neutral" limit has a writing system built into it.

**Mechanism as argument (P1, G3).** The work is nothing but four counts of the same string, displayed live: graphemes via `Intl.Segmenter` (the Unicode UAX #29 definition of what the eye reads as one character), code points via `Array.from`, UTF-16 units via `String.length` (what the classic counter actually tolls), and UTF-8 bytes via `TextEncoder` (what the wire carries). The remaining-characters meter is deliberately wired to `String.length`, the naive counter's real behaviour, so the bar goes red faster than your eye can justify. The disagreement is not illustrated by the mechanism — the disagreement *is* the mechanism. Nothing is decorative; remove any count and the argument is gone.

**Reference it answers (P2, P6).** This is Marcel Duchamp's *infra-mince* — the immeasurable, almost-nothing gap between two all-but-identical things — relocated into the platform character counter and made into a live instrument you operate yourself. It also answers the measurement tradition (Mel Bochner) by measuring the same object four ways and refusing to reconcile them, and it points squarely at value and authenticity (P6): the "unit" that gates public speech turns out to be contested and biased. It is materially distinct from the catalogue's other Unicode piece, *COPY / COPY* (byte-level difference between identically-rendered strings), and from *La Disparition* (removing a letter): here nothing is hidden and nothing removed — the string is fully visible, and it is the *counting of it* that fractures.

**Voice and ethics (P3, P7, P8, P9).** The build is the smallest that completes the thought: one editable line, four numbers, one honest meter, a wall label. The casual visitor gets a dry joke — "one emoji, eleven characters, that'll be extra" — while the literate visitor gets a precise point about how a technical unit encodes a politics of who gets counted, and who gets charged.
