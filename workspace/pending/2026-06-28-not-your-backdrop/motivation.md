# Not Your Backdrop

In 1958 Mark Rothko accepted a commission to paint murals for the Four Seasons, the luxury restaurant in Mies and Johnson's Seagram Building. He worked in deep oxblood and black, then visited the room, watched the wealthy eat beneath where his paintings would hang, and pulled out — returning the fee rather than let his work become the décor of a place "where the richest bastards in New York will come to feed and show off." The refusal is the artwork's true subject: a painting that would sooner disappear than serve as someone else's wallpaper.

The web has one exact analogue for "hung as decoration on premises that aren't yours": the **iframe**. Embed a page in your own site and it becomes a backdrop, a content-block, a garnish framed by your layout. So this work withdraws the instant it detects it has been framed. A single line — `window.self !== window.top` (with a `try/catch` because a cross-origin parent throws, which also means *framed*) — decides whether you are looking at the painting or at its refusal. Hung directly, it mixes a fresh oxblood field per visit (P5, the chance side) and asks to be seen dim, at arm's length, as Rothko demanded. Embedded anywhere else, it goes to near-black and states that it declines to be your décor. **The frame-bust *is* the withdrawal** (P1): not a picture of refusal but the act of it, performed live, only possible on the web (G1).

To make the gesture legible without sending you elsewhere, the page embeds a perfect copy of *itself*. The "Hang it in a restaurant" button captures the document's own source and feeds it to an `<iframe srcdoc>` — inline, no network request (P8) — captioned as the Four Seasons. The copy runs, finds itself framed, and goes dark. You watch the masterpiece installed in the fancy restaurant and switch off. This self-embedding is also a small Benjamin joke: the mechanically reproduced copy is materially identical to the original, yet the moment it is reproduced *as decoration* it is worth nothing, shows nothing — the aura was never in the pixels but in the context the artist refused.

It reads at two depths (P7): the casual viewer gets a clean gag — the great painting that blanks itself when you try to use it as a backdrop — while the literate viewer gets the Seagram story crossed with net.art's oldest reflex, the frame-buster. It answers Rothko's *Seagram Murals* (1958–59) directly (P2), sits on the order side of the chance/order axis as a deliberate act of refusal (P5), and aims at value and authenticity through the back door of *context* rather than price (P6-adjacent). The voice is the cheerful cynic, not the moralist: it doesn't lecture you about commodification, it just quietly turns off the lights when you sit down to dinner (P9). Smallest build that completes the thought (P3): two CSS forms, one conditional, one self-referential iframe.

**References:** Mark Rothko, *Seagram Murals* (1958–59) and the withdrawn Four Seasons commission, 1959 · net.art frame-busting · Walter Benjamin, *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1935).
