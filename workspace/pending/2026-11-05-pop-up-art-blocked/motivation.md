**Pop-up Art (Blocked)** takes the pun literally and lets the browser finish the argument. On load the page calls `window.open()` without asking, the way pop-up ads always did. Almost every modern browser refuses, and `window.open()` returns `null`. The page reports the refusal as a verdict: Pop Art, uninvited, has been classified as advertising and blocked. If you click "Allow Pop-up Art", a small window opens at a random spot on the screen. It is written locally into `about:blank` and dressed as a 2003 banner ad with Ben-Day dots ("Congratulations! You have been selected to receive ART"). It runs through Richard Hamilton's 1957 definition of Pop Art, *Popular, Transient, Expendable, Low cost, Mass produced, Young, Witty, Sexy, Gimmicky, Glamorous, Big business*, and strikes out one word per second. After eleven seconds it calls `window.close()` on itself.

The mechanism is the argument (P1, G3). Hamilton defined Pop as advertising's own qualities taken into art. The pop-up ad was the web's purest version of that: mass-produced, low cost, gimmicky, transient, big business. It is also the one popular form that culture voted to suppress, with pop-up blockers built into browsers by default from about 2000 to 2004. So the work does not illustrate censorship. It uses the real censor. Your browser's built-in heuristic is the jury, and `null` is its ruling (P6). The work also enacts the words "transient" and "expendable" literally: the window destroys itself, nothing is stored, and the next one is a fresh mass-produced copy with a new random "visitor number" and palette (P4).

It is a digital readymade at two levels (P2). The readymades are Hamilton's list and the pop-up ad format, and the browser's blocker turns them into a value judgement. It is double-coded (P7): a casual viewer gets a pun and an obnoxious flashing ad, while a literate viewer gets a question about consent. Pop needed no consent from its audience. Popular art online now has to ask for it, and that undoes what made it "popular" in the first place. Its place in the twenty-year argument: it answers y-a-v-a's *Pop Whaam!* (2020) by moving Pop out of the image and into browser policy, and it sits next to *The Agreeing Internet* (2009) as a portrait of the web's built-in manners. It is a minimal build (P3): one page, one `window.open`, one self-closing window, no network, no storage, no cookies, CC BY-SA (P8).
