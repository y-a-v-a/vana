# Rubrica (Copies Black)

A page of black prose in which the words that "matter" are red — and a box below it that proves the red is a lie of the glass. Select the passage, copy it, paste it: only the scribe's black letters arrive. The rubric never travels. It was never in the text.

The red is not markup. It is painted by the **CSS Custom Highlight API**: each rubricated word is a JavaScript `Range` registered in `CSS.highlights` and coloured by a `::highlight(rubrica)` rule. These marks exist only in the render layer — absent from the DOM (the passage has zero child elements), absent from `textContent`, and dropped by the clipboard. The page attests this live: *letters the rubricator added to the DOM: 0*. This enacts **P1 — concept is execution**: the mechanism's literal property (a highlight is not text and cannot be copied) *is* the argument about what reproduction can and cannot carry. Nothing is illustrated; the loss is real and demonstrable in the reader's own hands.

It answers a specific history. The **rubricator** was the second hand in the medieval scriptorium — after the scribe laid down the black, the rubricator came with red ochre (*rubrica*) to mark the feast day, the initial, the name of value. Gutenberg's **42-line Bible** (c. 1455) left the spaces blank, still expecting that hand. Here the browser is the rubricator, and it works for free, per visit, over letters it did not add. This is **P2 — the digital readymade**: a new web-platform primitive appropriated to re-stage an eight-hundred-year-old division of labour.

Under it runs **Walter Benjamin** (*The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction*, 1935). What the copy carries is the scribe's black work — "entire and equal and cheap." What it cannot carry is the mark that said *this one matters*. The last line delivers the thesis as a joke (**P9**): the aura, it turns out, was a highlight. A casual reader gets the surface gag — red words that won't copy — without a wall text (**P7**); a literate reader gets the reproduction argument underneath.
