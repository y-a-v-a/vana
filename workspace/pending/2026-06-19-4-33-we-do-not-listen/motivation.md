# 4′33″ (We Do Not Listen)

John Cage's *4′33″* (1952) opens the ears: the performer touches nothing, and the ambient sound of the room — coughs, rain on the roof, the audience's own unease — becomes the piece. It is the canonical readymade of sound, indeterminate by design (P5, chance). This work re-stages it as the one instrument every visitor already owns: the browser's media transport. A play button, a progress bar, three movements marked *tacet* with the premiere's durations (30″ · 2′23″ · 1′40″), a clock counting to 4:33. Press Listen and the page performs exactly that long a silence.

The twist that keeps this from being reverent imitation (avoiding the homage anti-pattern) is *why* the silence is silent. A web page **could** open the microphone and play your room back to you — that would be the literal, faithful, surveillant *4′33″*. This one refuses. It creates no `AudioContext`, requests no `getUserMedia`, schedules nothing to make a sound, and sends nothing anywhere. Cage's instruction to listen to your environment is honoured by the only means a privacy-respecting page has: getting out of the way. The transport runs a real, timed performance of nothing. **The mechanism is the argument** (P1): the silence you hear is the literal sound of the page not recording you.

So the work double-codes (P7). A casual visitor sees a music player that plays nothing for four and a half minutes — a dry joke. A literate visitor recognises *4′33″* and Tudor's Woodstock score. And the privacy-aware visitor hears the real subject: the y-a-v-a ethic — no cookies, no trackers, no microphone, no network (P8) — has stopped being delivery and become content. "We do not search, we serve" turns into "we do not listen." The smallest build that completes the thought (P3): three timed `tacet` movements, a `requestAnimationFrame` clock, and an audio engine deliberately left empty.

It answers John Cage, *4′33″* (1952), and sits on the chance/order axis (P5) by inverting it: Cage's indeterminacy was the unpredictable room let *in*; here the indeterminacy is the unheard, unrecorded room kept *out*. A node in the twenty-year argument that the internet already embodies 20th-century art's gestures — and that its default machinery of capture is precisely what conceptual silence now has to refuse.
