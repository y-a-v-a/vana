# Indivisible

Three words that mean *not to be broken* — **indivisible**, **unbreakable**, **continuous** — are each placed in a column narrower than the word itself. Faced with a line it cannot fit, the browser's layout engine consults a per-language hyphenation dictionary it ships internally, and breaks the word at a point of its own choosing. The word that says it cannot be divided is divided; the hyphen that does the dividing exists nowhere in the text you wrote.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). This is not a picture of a broken word — it is a word broken, live, by the engine, in front of you. The hyphen is inserted at render time from a dictionary the reader cannot open; it is not a character in the DOM. The readback panel proves it: `textContent` holds zero soft hyphens (U+00AD), zero hyphens (U+2010), zero hyphen-minus (U+002D) — yet the screen is full of hyphens. Select a word and copy it and the hyphen does not come with you. It lives only on the glass. That is a **digital readymade** (**P2**): the appropriated object is the browser's own typesetting behaviour, recontextualised as an editorial act.

The piece sits on the chance/order axis (**P5**). The author's word is *order*; the break point is decided by forces the author does not control — the viewport width and the declared `lang` attribute. Resize the window and the breaks migrate; switch the language buttons and the *same* word breaks at *different* points, because a different nationality's dictionary is now in charge. Every reader, at every width, in every declared tongue, reads a differently-broken poem, and none of them can find the seam. The whole word and the broken word differ by a mark that is there and not there — Marcel Duchamp's **infrathin**.

It answers **concrete poetry** — Augusto de Campos and the Brazilian Noigandres group of the 1950s, who fought for total control of the word as an object fixed in space ("verbivocovisual"). On the web that control is surrendered by default: the poet no longer decides where the line, or even the word, breaks. The engine does, silently, and re-decides on every reflow. Double-coded (**P7**): the casual viewer gets the gag — a machine snapping words that beg not to be snapped — while the literate viewer reads a small argument about authorship, typesetting, and who holds the pen once the text reaches the glass.

Smallest build that completes the thought (**P3**): one CSS property (`hyphens: auto`), narrow columns, and a readback. No image, no font, no request, no storage. CC BY-SA 4.0, attributed to y-a-v-a and to the works it answers.
