# Fountain (As Manufactured)

In 1917 Marcel Duchamp bought a factory-made urinal, turned it ninety degrees, signed it "R. Mutt," titled it *Fountain*, and submitted it to a jury-free exhibition that hid it anyway. The scandal was never craft — Duchamp made nothing. The gesture was *selection plus reorientation plus signature*. This work asks: what is the web's true readymade? Not an image, not a file. It is the **native form control** — the `<input type="range">` that every browser ships, that none of us built, whose exact appearance is decided by the manufacturer. So I took one, left it completely unstyled, rotated it ninety degrees, and signed it R. Mutt.

The mechanism *is* the argument (P1, P2). The one hard rule of the piece is a refusal: the control receives no CSS — no colour, no `::-webkit-slider-thumb`, no track styling. What you see is therefore authored entirely by your browser vendor, exactly as the porcelain of Duchamp's urinal was authored by the Trenton sanitary-ware works and not by Duchamp. The provenance line names the manufacturer it detects (Apple/WebKit, Google/Blink, Mozilla/Gecko…), read locally from the UA string and sent nowhere. Every visitor receives a *different cast* of the same readymade — a multiplicity impossible for a print, which settles G1: this could not exist on paper, because on paper the object would finally have a single fixed manufacturer, me.

It answers Duchamp's *Fountain* (1917) directly and stays double-coded (P7): the casual viewer gets the gag of a slider stood on its end and signed by a plumber-pseudonym; the literate viewer gets the claim that the browser's default widget is the readymade the web forgot it was making. The wall-label apparatus (P6) carries the second historical fact — the Society of Independent Artists' "anyone who pays may exhibit" rule, which the open web inherited and then betrayed by keeping nothing else.

Minimal build, one gesture (P3): a native element, one `transform: rotate(90deg)`, a signature, a label. On the chance/order axis it leans to *chance* in a new place — not databending or randomness, but the manufacturer's chance, the fact that the artist cannot govern how the object arrives. Dry, cheerful-cynic (P9): the joke is real and the idea under it is that authorship on the web was always outsourced. Open licence, no cookies, no trackers, no network (P8).
