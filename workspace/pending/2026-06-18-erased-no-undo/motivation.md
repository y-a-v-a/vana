# Erased (No Undo)

In 1953 Robert Rauschenberg asked Willem de Kooning for a drawing he could erase. De Kooning, understanding the trap, handed over a dense one in several media that he knew he would miss. Rauschenberg spent about a month and a drawer of erasers removing it, then framed the near-blank sheet with a gold label. The artwork is not the drawing and not the blank paper — it is the *act of erasure*, and the authorship that quietly transfers in the doing.

This page re-stages that gesture as a thing only a browser can do. On first visit it generates a dense graphite drawing — laborious, gestural, worth missing — and hands it to you with one instruction: erase it. Erasing is real destructive canvas compositing (`globalCompositeOperation = 'destination-out'`); the graphite you remove is gone. The mechanism **is** the argument (P1, P3): the artwork is made by destroying the artwork, exactly as Rauschenberg's was, and you cannot make it without committing the loss yourself.

The web's defining promise is the *undo* — Ctrl+Z, the back button, the cached original always one keystroke away. Here that promise is withheld: the erased state is written to `localStorage` and never reset, so reloading shows your ghost, not a fresh drawing, and Ctrl+Z only summons a dry refusal. The single most web-native expectation a viewer brings is the one the work denies, which is what makes the irreversibility land. This is a digital readymade of de Kooning's marks (P2), staged with the apparatus of value and authorship — gold frame, wall label, "signed over to you" (P6) — and it sits on the chance/order axis (P5): the drawing arrives by chance (a seeded random hand), and your willed, ordered erasure undoes it.

It answers Rauschenberg's *Erased de Kooning Drawing* (1953) and, behind it, Duchamp's readymade — but with a twist rather than reverence (P9). There is no de Kooning here to sacrifice, and there hasn't been one since 1953; the page admits this and draws a stand-in for you to destroy, turning a one-time act of art-historical vandalism into a quiet, repeatable, per-device ritual. Following Rauschenberg, completion stops at a ghost, not a void: ~7% of the marks survive, because the trace is the work. CC BY-SA 4.0, no cookies, no trackers, no network — only your own erasure, kept on your own machine, and not given back.
