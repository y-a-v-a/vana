# Incipit (Does Not Copy)

Before the printing press, the opening letter of a manuscript — the *versal*, the historiated initial — was the single most laborious gesture in the book: gold-leafed, hand-painted, never twice the same. It was the aura made visible, the part of the page that could not be dictated or duplicated. This work rebuilds that letter in the browser and then reveals that the web has quietly inherited the medieval split between the unique image and the reproducible word.

The mechanism is the argument (**P1**). The illuminated capital here is a CSS `::first-letter` / `initial-letter` pseudo-element. A pseudo-element can *style* the opening glyph as extravagantly as it likes — gild it, sink it three lines, wrap it in a lattice ground — but by specification it **cannot add a single character to the document**. So the gold exists only in the render. Select the manuscript, copy it, paste it anywhere: the words arrive (identical for everyone, aura-less, infinitely reproducible), and the illumination stays on the glass. The button "Show me what leaves this page" prints the manuscript's own `textContent` — plain letters, no gold — staging Walter Benjamin's *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1935) as a live browser fact rather than a wall text (**P7**): the reproduction is faithful and the aura simply does not come along.

The chance/order axis runs through the versal itself (**P5**). Its palette is minted per visit from `crypto.getRandomValues`, held in memory and saved nowhere, so each illuminated capital is a true one-off original — the aura returns, for exactly one page-load, and then is gone on reload. The ordered part (the interlace lattice, the gilt gradient, the sunk drop-cap geometry) is a fixed rule; the coloured light poured over it is chance. This doubles the concept: within one page you get both regimes at once — the reproducible, transmissible text and the unique, untransmissible ornament — and the `::first-letter` mechanism is what forces them apart, because it can only ever dress a glyph, never become one.

It is a digital readymade of a manuscript form (**P2**), built as the smallest page that completes the thought (**P3**) — one paragraph, one pseudo-element, a dozen lines of script. It is fully self-contained: no fonts, no network, no cookies, no storage, no trackers (**P8**), released under CC BY-SA 4.0 with attribution to y-a-v-a and to the referenced tradition and text. The reference it answers most directly is the medieval scriptorium, a pre-print ancestor the catalogue had not yet named, reached through Benjamin — and the dry punchline is the wall label's own medium line: *pixels on screen (gold not included)*.
