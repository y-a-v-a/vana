# Print Gallery (The Part It Cannot Draw)

M. C. Escher's lithograph *Prentententoonstelling* (1956) shows a man in a gallery looking at a print of a harbour town that curves around and contains the very gallery he is standing in — a picture that swallows itself. At the centre Escher placed a circular blank patch with his signature: the one spot where the logarithmic geometry would not close, the part he could not draw. (Hendrik Lenstra's team finished the maths in 2003; Escher never did.) This page does what a lithograph cannot: it hangs an actual, live copy of itself inside itself, and keeps going.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). An empty `<iframe>` is fed the document's own `outerHTML` via `srcdoc` — a digital readymade (**P2**) built from nothing but the page and the browser. Each nested copy runs the same script, captures its own source, and hangs another copy one level deeper, shrinking and twisting `scale(0.82) rotate(4.2deg)` into a Droste spiral. Self-containment here is not depicted, it is *real*: the picture genuinely contains the picture. And it stops — at a fixed depth the recursion refuses, and the frame is left blank. That blank is not a design choice; it is the medium's own finite nesting limit standing in for Escher's unpaintable centre. The machine, like the artist, gives up in exactly the same place.

It sits on the **order** pole of the chance/order axis (**P5**): pure deterministic recursion, identical in every browser, every visit — no randomness, no network, nothing stored (**P4**, live per-visit render from source alone). The build is the smallest that completes the thought (**P3**): one iframe, one `outerHTML`, one depth check. It double-codes (**P7**) — a casual viewer gets an infinite hall-of-mirrors zoom; a literate one gets self-reference, the Droste effect, and computability's edge rendered as a hole you can point at.

It answers earlier y-a-v-a works that read the page's own source (*The Original*, 2026) and that repurpose `<iframe srcdoc>` (*Not Your Backdrop*, 2026), but turns the mechanism inward: not download, not refusal, but recursion — the web as the one medium where an image can truly hold itself, right up to the point where it can't.
