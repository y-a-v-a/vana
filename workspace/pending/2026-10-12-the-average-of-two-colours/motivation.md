# The Average of Two Colours

Ask a child: blue and yellow make green. Ask a screen: blue and yellow make a murky grey. Ask the screen *again*, politely naming a colour space, and it makes green after all — or teal, or violet. This page puts two native colour pickers at the top and, below them, ten swatches that each compute the **exact same 50 % mix of the exact same two colours** — differing only in the interpolation space named after CSS `color-mix(in …)`. They refuse to agree. The piece is that refusal. The "average" of two colours turns out not to be a fact you discover but a place you decide to stand: `srgb` gives you the gamma-muddied grey everyone complains about, `srgb-linear` mixes light the way physics does, `oklab` lands where the eye expects, and the polar spaces (`hsl`, `lch`, `oklch`) will even hand you two different middles depending on which way round the wheel you walk.

**Mechanism as argument (P1).** Nothing on the page is painted by hand. The one line of JavaScript does nothing but copy the two picker values into two CSS custom properties; every midpoint is then produced by the browser's own standards-defined interpolation math. So the disagreement is not an illustration of a point about colour — it *is* the browser, live, computing ten incompatible "halfways" from identical ends, and you can drag either endpoint and watch all ten quarrel in real time. That live, engine-driven computation is also why it could never be a print (**G1**): a print would freeze one arbitrary middle and quietly pretend it was *the* middle, which is exactly the lie the work is about. It sits firmly on the **order** pole of the chance↔order axis (**P5**) — pure deterministic spec arithmetic, no randomness — and it reads at two depths (**P7**): a pretty strip of colour for the passer-by, a small demolition of "objective" averaging for anyone who reads the labels. The build is the smallest that completes the thought (**P3**), and the voice is the dry cheerful cynic (**P9**): *blue and yellow make green; also grey; also this.*

**What it answers.** It answers Josef Albers' *Interaction of Color* (1963) and Chevreul's 1839 law of simultaneous contrast — the whole tradition that colour has no fixed identity, only relations — but relocates the relativity from the *viewer's eye* to the *machine's definition of a midpoint*. Where Albers showed one colour looking like two, this shows one "average" being ten. It also stages the old Goethe-versus-Newton argument about whether colour is physics or perception by letting you literally click between `srgb-linear` (Newton's light) and `oklab` (perception, after Björn Ottosson's 2020 OKLab) and see them disagree; and it nods to Duchamp's *infrathin* — the in-between so thin it was supposed to be nothing, here revealed as a contested territory the browser can't pick a point in.

**Lineage.** It belongs to the long colour thread in the catalogue — *Optical Mixture* (Seurat, dissolving dots by zoom), *Interaction of Color* (Albers, same RGB on two grounds), *148 Colours* (Richter, the CSS named list) — but is materially distinct from each: none of those touches `color-mix()` or the claim that the *midpoint itself is undefined*. This is the colour-theory argument turned on the one operation everyone assumes is trivial — splitting the difference — and finding there is no difference to split until you name a space to split it in.
