Duchamp's defence of *Fountain* in *The Blind Man* (1917) reduces the readymade to one act: *he chose it*. The web already has a single, un-restylable, OS-drawn instrument whose only function is choosing an ordinary article of life: `<input type="file">`. This page is that instrument on a plinth. Pick anything on your disk — a tax return, a selfie, a half-finished novel — and a museum cartel appears: title from the filename, date from `lastModified`, medium from the MIME type, dimensions in bytes, artist: you, by choice.

The mechanism is the argument (P1, P2). The page never opens the file: no FileReader, no `arrayBuffer()`, "bytes looked at: 0". Its useful significance disappears, exactly as Duchamp demanded, because the work refuses to look at what it is, only at its label. Better still, the provenance line is filled in by the browser itself: the HTML Standard requires `input.value` to report the path as `C:\fakepath\` + filename, on Windows, macOS and Linux alike. The browser removes where the object came from as a privacy rule and puts in a fake origin. Provenance, which is how the art market makes things worth something (P6), comes back as a fake required by the spec. Nobody had to write that joke.

Double-coded (P7): the casual viewer sees their `IMG_4031.jpg` turned into a gallery piece; the literate viewer sees the readymade's decontextualisation built into a W3C/WHATWG sandbox rule, and an institution (the cartel) authenticating something it never inspected. It is minimal (P3): one native control, one handler, one label. It is live (P4), because nothing exists until a visitor chooses, and the nomination lapses on reload. It is ethical in its delivery (P8): nothing is read, stored or sent, and the counter "bytes sent: 0" is the page being honest about that.

In the catalogue it answers *Fountain (As Manufactured)* (the vendor-drawn widget as readymade) and *Cartel* (the label as the work) by moving the act of choosing to the visitor. It also answers *Provenance* (2026), where the privacy web blacked out the chain of custody. Here the browser does more than erase: it makes up a fake origin.
