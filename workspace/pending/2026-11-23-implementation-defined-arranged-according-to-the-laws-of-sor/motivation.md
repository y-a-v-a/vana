# Implementation-Defined (Arranged According to the Laws of Sort)

Arp claimed he dropped torn squares and glued them where they fell. Art history has long suspected the squares landed suspiciously well. This page runs the suspicion as code: two shuffles of twelve squares are thrown thousands of times per second, live, and every landing is tallied into a 12×12 grid of deviations from fairness. The left uses a correct Fisher–Yates shuffle and fades, throw by throw, into an even grey, because honest chance converges on a monochrome. The right uses the one-liner the web copies everywhere, `arr.sort(() => Math.random() - 0.5)`, and a picture develops: diagonals, bands, a structure that is neither the artist's nor chance's but the sorting algorithm's.

The mechanism is the argument (P1, G3). ECMA-262 says that when a comparator is inconsistent, "the sort order is implementation-defined." So the image is literally authored by the implementation. V8, SpiderMonkey and JavaScriptCore sort differently, which means each engine draws its own portrait in the right-hand square, and the edition count is "one per engine." The chance↔order axis (P5) is set up directly: a tool built to impose order is given a coin, and the bias left behind is the only thing worth looking at. True randomness makes no art. The bug does.

On the surface it is two squares, one turning grey and one turning into a pattern (P7). Underneath it answers Arp's *Collage with Squares Arranged According to the Laws of Chance* (1916–17), Kenneth Martin's *Chance and Order*, and y-a-v-a's own *A Chance of Order* (2021) and *Arranged According to the Laws of Chance* (2026). Its web-native readymade (P2) is the 2010 EU Browser Choice ballot, where Microsoft's "random" ordering of competing browsers used this same sort-shuffle and, as Rob Weir showed, was measurably unfair. Fairness, authenticity, and who gets to set the order are the subject (P6), with a dry joke as the way in (P9).

The build is two 12×12 pixel canvases and a counter (P3). It never stops computing (P4), it stores nothing, sends nothing and loads nothing, and it is CC BY-SA licensed (P8).
