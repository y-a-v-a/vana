# Anonyme Skulpturen (o. O.)

Bernd and Hilla Becher spent fifty years driving across Europe to photograph water towers, gas holders and winding towers — always frontally, always at the same distance, in flat overcast light — and pinned them up in deadpan grids. They called them *Anonymous Sculptures*. The method **is** the argument: identical framing plus systematic variation plus the grid forces the eye to compare, and comparison reveals that these anonymous functional structures have a formal grammar. It was also a race against demolition — an archive built to outlast its own subjects.

This page keeps the Bechers' framing exactly (one shared horizon, one fixed distance, one grid of twelve) and inverts everything else. Each water tower is drawn procedurally in your browser at the instant the page loads, from a strict rule-set filled by chance — a lattice, pier or single shaft carrying a cylinder, bulb or cup, roofed flat, coned or domed. The plate is held only in RAM. There is no storage, no cookie, no network request, no seed. **Reload demolishes every tower on the page, and none can ever be re-photographed.** The Bechers fought forgetting with the fixity of the negative; this is the same typology run backwards — an infinite anti-archive of sculptures that never had a location (*o. O.*, ohne Ort) or a date (*o. J.*, ohne Jahr), and that are gone the moment you look away.

This enacts **P1** — the mechanism is the message, not decoration: the grid of identical frames *is* the Becher comparison, and the per-visit, memory-only generation *is* the anti-archive. It is a **P2** digital readymade of a photographic method rather than a single picture, and it lives on the **P5** chance/order axis with unusual clarity: the frame, distance and grid are pure order; the forms inside them are pure chance. It runs live and un-fixable (**P4**), it is **P7** double-coded (a casual viewer reads a tidy typological plate; a literate one reads Walter Benjamin's reproduction-and-archive problem stood on its head), and it keeps the **P8** ethics — CC BY-SA, no trackers, nothing persisted.

Named references it answers: **Bernd & Hilla Becher, *Typologies / Anonyme Skulpturen* (Water Towers)** and the Düsseldorf School's deadpan archive; and **Walter Benjamin, *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1935)** — where the Bechers' camera guaranteed the reproducible, comparable image, and this page refuses reproducibility outright: it cannot be re-shot, so every visitor is the sole and final witness of twelve sculptures that were demolished before anyone else arrived.
