# International Klein Blue (If You Can Find It)

A monochrome painting that begins gessoed and unpainted. To give it a colour, the
viewer presses *Take a colour* and the browser's native **EyeDropper API** opens — a tool
whose sole function is to reach *past* this page and sample a single pixel from anywhere on
the screen: the desktop, another window, a photograph of a sky. Whatever it lifts floods the
canvas and is recorded on the wall label as "Appropriated light, #RRGGBB… somewhere that is
not this painting." Press *Return it* and the pigment is given back; the work keeps nothing.
Reload and it forgets. Where the browser has no eyedropper, the painting simply stays
unpainted — and says so.

The conceptual point is about the ownership of colour. Yves Klein patented *International
Klein Blue* and staged his career on the fiction that a colour could belong to an artist.
This painting owns no pigment at all: every colour it can ever wear is, by construction,
stolen from something else on the viewer's screen. It is the anti-Klein and the readymade
for colour at once — Duchamp's gesture (P2, the digital readymade) performed on light rather
than on a urinal or a bottle rack.

The **mechanism is the argument** (P1, P3). The EyeDropper API cannot invent a colour and it
cannot copy one from within the artwork; it can *only* cross the frame and appropriate from
the world outside. So the single act available to the viewer — the act that makes the
painting a painting — is literally an act of appropriation. Nothing here is decoration bolted
onto a theme: to paint this work you must take, and the tool the browser hands you is a tool
that only takes. This also distinguishes it materially from the catalogue's *Pure Colour*
(which *invents* a colour via the OS picker) and *The Blackest Black* (which *copies* a fixed
hex to the clipboard): those choose and duplicate; this one steals from elsewhere.

It sits on the chance side of the chance/order axis (P5): the palette is entirely contingent
on whatever happens to be on your screen when you look, so no two visits and no two viewers
paint the same picture. And it keeps the delivery ethic (P8): open licence stated on the
page, no cookies, no storage, no network, no server — the whole work is a single
self-contained document that appropriates in RAM and repents on reload.

**References answered:** Yves Klein, *IKB* (owning a colour); Marcel Duchamp, the readymade
(found material recontextualised); and the web-native **EyeDropper API** (WICG) as the
found object itself — browser plumbing whose literal operation is appropriation.
Cheerful-cynic tone (P9): the museum label calls each colour a "theft" and counts them.
