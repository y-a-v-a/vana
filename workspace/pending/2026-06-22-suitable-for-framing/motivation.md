# Suitable for Framing

A clean little painting sits matted and framed on screen: a luminous colour field, mixed fresh for every visit, breathing slowly because it is made of light and light does not hold still. Below it, a straight gallery placard — *Medium: light on screen. Edition: one per visit.* — and a single instruction: **Try to print it.** Do, and the page hands back an almost-empty sheet bearing one line: *this work is made of light and does not survive paper.* The frame, the painting, the placard, the colophon — all of it vanishes in the print pipeline by design.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). The whole work is a `@media print` rule that sets `body * { display:none }` and lets only a `body::after` epitaph through. Nothing illustrates the idea "a web-native artwork cannot be reproduced on paper" — the CSS print stylesheet *performs* that disappearance the instant you reach for Ctrl/Cmd-P or "Save as PDF." This is the DNA's own first gate (G1, "would it die as a print?") staged as the artwork itself: the answer is yes, and you can watch it happen.

It answers two references by inverting them. Walter Benjamin's *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1935) worried that the copy strips the aura from the original; here the reproduction returns **nothing at all** — the aura is the live, breathing screen and the paper copy is null (**P2**, **P6** on value and authenticity). And Seth Siegelaub's catalogue-as-exhibition — the *Xerox Book* (1968), where the printed page literally *was* the gallery — is turned exactly inside out: now the page is the gallery and printing closes it.

It sits on the chance/order axis (**P5**) at the chance end: a per-visit random hue pair, computed client-side, so no two viewings frame the same painting and none can be pinned to a sheet. The voice is the cheerful cynic (**P9**): the placard says *Suitable for Framing*, the oldest compliment paid to a printable picture, then quietly refuses to be framed. A casual viewer gets the gag — *the artwork that breaks your printer's heart* — and a literate viewer gets the Benjamin/Siegelaub argument underneath (**P7**), with no wall text needed for the surface to land.

Smallest build that completes the thought (**P3**): one HTML file, a few CSS rules carrying the entire concept, ten lines of JS for the per-visit colour. No cookies, no trackers, no network, no storage — the work is constitutionally screen-only and constitutionally private (**P8**).
