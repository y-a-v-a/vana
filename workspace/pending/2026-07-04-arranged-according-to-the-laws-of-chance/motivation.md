# Arranged According to the Laws of Chance

In 1916–17 Hans (Jean) Arp tore paper into squares, dropped them onto a sheet, and glued them where they fell — handing composition over to gravity and accident so that the work would owe nothing to taste, skill, or the ego of the maker. This page does the same, but abdicates authorship to a purer and stranger source of chance: `crypto.getRandomValues()`, the browser's cryptographic entropy — the randomness banks trust with their keys — spent here on scattering torn paper across a cream ground. Every position, size, rotation and deckled edge is a throw the artist never took.

The concept **is** the execution (**P1**). The piece sits on the chance pole of the chance↔order axis the DNA keeps returning to (**P5**), and it answers the order works in the catalogue directly — *A Chance of Order* (Kenneth Martin, strict rules) and *Random Distribution of π* (Morellet, deterministic and byte-identical in every browser) — by being their exact opposite. Where those are reproducible, this is not: there is **no seed**, and **nothing is stored** (no cookie, no `localStorage`, no network). The arrangement is drawn from hardware entropy and held only in RAM, so a reload loses it forever. That is the mechanism arguing, not decorating: the choice of a *cryptographically secure* RNG over `Math.random()` is the whole point, because pseudo-randomness could be reproduced from its seed, and true entropy cannot. Reproduction fails here not by license but by physics.

This answers Walter Benjamin from the chance side (**P6**, value/authenticity). *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* worries that the copy dissolves the aura of the original; here the machine that is supposed to be the perfect copyist instead mints a picture that has no original to copy — each throw is a unique, un-recoverable event, unownable and unsellable because it cannot be pinned down. The readout states the medium plainly (*chance drawn: N bytes · seed: none · saved: nothing*), so the literate viewer gets the cryptographic argument while the casual viewer gets the surface gag: a Dada collage composed by "nobody in particular," the artist having recused himself (**P7**, double-coding; **P9**, the wit carries the idea).

It could not survive as a print (**G1**): a print fixes the one arrangement the work exists specifically to refuse to fix, and destroys the irreproducibility that is its subject. Named references: **Hans Arp**, *Untitled (Collage with Squares Arranged According to the Laws of Chance)*, 1916–17; the Dada gesture of chance operations; **Walter Benjamin**, *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1935); and, on the web-native lineage, the deterministic chance-and-order pieces already in the catalogue that this one throws against.
