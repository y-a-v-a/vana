# Entropy (Black Sand, White Sand)

Robert Smithson gave conceptual art its clearest thermodynamic parable: a sandbox divided into black sand and white sand, a child made to run clockwise until the halves grey out, then made to run anticlockwise — which does not restore the division but only deepens the grey ("Entropy and the New Monuments," 1966). This work *is* that sandbox, served live. The left half is black, the right half white, and dragging the pointer "runs" in the box: each motion performs hundreds of blind, sand-conserving neighbour-swaps. No grain is added or removed, yet the seam never comes back.

The mechanism is the argument (P1). The swap operation is mathematically reversible per-step and conserves the amount of each sand exactly — and that is precisely why it is irreversible in practice. The "Disorder" plate counts black/white adjacencies in real time and only ever climbs toward its ceiling; the "run anticlockwise" button is offered as if direction could undo, and of course it raises the number too. The piece does not *illustrate* the second law of thermodynamics with a pretty animation — it submits to it. The only restoration on offer is "begin the universe again" (reload), which is not a correction but a new cosmos.

It answers a named work and sits squarely on the chance/order axis the DNA cares about (P5): order is the initial clean division, chance is the diffusion that eats it, and the work stages the tension as a thing you do with your hand rather than watch. As a digital readymade (P2) the found object is the arrow of time itself — entropy — appropriated through the most ordinary web material, a pointer drag over a canvas. It could not be a print: a print is the one thing entropy forbids, a fixed and final division.

The voice is the cheerful cynic (P9): a gallery wall-label politely informs you that the undo button you were promised by the entire interactive web does not, thermodynamically, exist. Open licence, no cookies, no storage, no network (P8) — nothing persists because, per Smithson, nothing here is meant to be conserved except the sand. References answered: Robert Smithson, *Entropy and the New Monuments* (1966) and his sandbox parable; the net.art tradition of the browser canvas as material.
