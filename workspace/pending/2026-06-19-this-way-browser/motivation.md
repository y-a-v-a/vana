# this way browser

Stanley Brouwn made distance the medium: he counted his own footsteps across cities, recorded the figures in his own units, and sealed the results in closed file boxes — refusing photography, refusing reproduction, insisting the measured walk could never be shown, only attested. *this way browser* takes that exact gesture and gives it the one walker the web actually owns: your pointer. As you move, the page sums the euclidean distance your cursor travels and converts it, via the CSS reference pixel (1/96 inch, by definition), into real metres and into "steps." The number is the only artwork. The path is never drawn.

The mechanism *is* the argument (P1). A distance is a behaviour, not an image — so the work cannot be a print, cannot be screenshotted (a still frame captures none of the motion that *is* the piece), and is gone the instant you leave: nothing is stored, cached, or transmitted (P4, P8). On a touchscreen the walk becomes literal: there is no hover to coast on, so you must press a finger to the glass and drag your own body across it — Pointer Events unify mouse, pen and touch, and `touch-action: none` claims the gesture so the browser stops cancelling the journey to scroll. One walker, every device. This is the digital readymade (P2) of Brouwn's distance works, and it sits squarely on the chance/order axis (P5): the measuring rule is strict and deterministic, the journey it measures is your own aimless wandering. It answers a specific lineage — Brouwn's *this way brouwn* (1961) and the step/distance pieces — and the title pun (Brouwn → browser) is the cheerful-cynic carrier wave (P9, P7): a "pedometer for your mouse" on the surface, a meditation on the unreproducible and unsellable on the inside.

The nuance is in the honesty of the lie. The conversion assumes a normalised screen that your screen is not; the measurement is exact and the truth is unknowable. The page declares the assumption in its own comments and counter and measures anyway — the gap between the precise number and the unknowable real distance is as much the work as the number is. Brouwn locked his figures in a box; this page locks them in the impossibility of reproduction, which is the most net-native vault there is (answering Benjamin's reproduction problem from the back). You walk, it counts, you leave, it forgets.
