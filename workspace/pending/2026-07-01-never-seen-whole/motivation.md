# 手卷 — Never Seen Whole

The Chinese handscroll (手卷, *shǒujuàn*) is the anti-museum format. It was designed to be unrolled between two hands, revealing only one arm's length of silk at a time, read from right to left as a journey through time and terrain, and then rolled shut. It was never hung, never framed, never taken in at a glance. The Western museum betrays this completely: it flattens the whole scroll behind glass, lit end to end, and calls the exposure "respect." This work restores the scroll to its true condition — because the browser viewport has no choice but to crop it.

The mechanism *is* the argument (P1). The page is a single horizontal-scroll container far wider than any viewport, opened on load at its rightmost edge — exactly where a handscroll begins. To see the ink ranges you must scroll leftward, and to see the far mountains you must roll the near ones out of sight; you physically cannot hold the whole thing open at once. A live readout confirms the constraint ("you see 12% at once, never more"). This is not a responsive-design limitation apologised for — it is the correct viewing apparatus, faithfully reproduced. The scroll ends, at the far left, on a colophon and a red collector's seal, mirroring the way real handscrolls accrue their provenance as an appended tail.

It is a digital readymade of a format rather than an image (P2), answering **Wang Ximeng's *A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains* (千里江山圖, 1113)** — a scroll so fragile that unrolling it is the very reason it is almost never exhibited. It sits on the chance/order axis (P5): the ranges follow a strict back-to-front rule of paleness and height (order), while everything else is minted per visit by `Math.random` (chance) — the very length of silk is cut anew each load (so no two scrolls run the same distance, exactly as no two real handscrolls do), the number of receding ranges, each ridgeline, and the drifting mist — then kept nowhere; reload rolls the silk back to blank. The build is deliberately the smallest that completes the thought (P3): no library, one canvas, one scroller, monochrome ink on warm paper.

It is double-coded (P7). A casual viewer gets a pleasant infinite ink-wash scroll; a literate one gets an argument about display ideology — that the museum's totalising glance is a modern imposition, and that a humble `overflow-x` is more faithful to a thousand-year-old painting than a vitrine. The cheerful-cynic register (P9) lives in the colophon: laying the scroll flat "and calling that respect." No print could carry the piece, because the instant you print it whole you have committed the exact betrayal it names (G1).

**References:** Wang Ximeng, *A Thousand Li of Rivers and Mountains* (1113); the Chinese handscroll (手卷) format and its right-to-left, arm's-length reading; net.art (the scroll axis as material).
