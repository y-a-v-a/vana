John Cage left one instruction for *ORGAN²/ASLSP*: play it "as slow as possible." Halberstadt answered with an organ, a church and 639 years (2001–2640), and the internet treats each rare chord change there as an event. This work answers the same instruction with the browser's own physics. ECMAScript defines time as ±8.64e15 ms around 1970: from 20 April 271,822 BC to 13 September 275,760 AD. Past that, `new Date()` returns `Invalid Date`. That range is the slowest the web can possibly go, so it becomes the length of the performance. The piece began before our species existed and is now just past halfway (about 49.6%, shown to twelve decimals so the last digits keep moving). It ends in a parse error, and the page prints that error live as its *Fine*.

The mechanism is the argument (P1). The score is 131,072 chords, one roughly every 4.18 years. Each is cast by a fixed hash of its segment index: Cage-style chance frozen into a deterministic score (P5, chance held inside order). Because the system clock is the only conductor, every browser on Earth plays the same chord at the same moment with no server, no stream and no sync protocol (P4, P8). Right now it is a single G4, held since April 2024, and the page names the next change (June 2028) and the chord that follows. Set your clock forward and you hear the future early. That is the honest weak point of any duration made from data, and the footer says so openly.

The surface reads at two depths (P7). The casual viewer gets "a song that lasts half a million years, and you missed the first 271,000." The literate viewer gets something else: on the timeline, Cage's monumental 639-year performance is the black tick by the red playhead, about one pixel wide. Institutional long-duration art shrinks to a rounding error against a spec value nobody chose for its beauty (P6, P9). Nothing is recorded, stored or sent. "Leave" closes the AudioContext, and the piece continues without you, because it never needed an audience: only a clock (P3, P4).

The work also answers y-a-v-a's own *The webpage going on and off* (2023). That piece was a render loop with no end. This one is a render loop whose end is fixed by the spec, and it is the most distant deadline the web can state.
