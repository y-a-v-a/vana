# The Obliteration Room (Please Cover Everything)

A pristine white room fills the browser. You are handed exactly one dot. Click any
surface and it appears; the crowd before and after you does the same, and the white
disappears under an accumulation of identical, anonymous marks. This is Yayoi Kusama's
*The Obliteration Room* (2002– ) — the white domestic interior a museum invites its
visitors to bury under coloured stickers — rebuilt as the web's most banal atom of
participation: the checkbox.

The whole piece is **pure HTML and CSS with no JavaScript at all**. Each surface of the
room is a `<label>` wrapping a hidden `<input type="checkbox">`; the CSS `:checked`
pseudo-class is the only thing that plants a dot. This is the argument, not the
decoration (**P1, P3**). The checkbox is the internet's unit of anonymous mass consent —
*I agree, I select, count me in* — and here that same tick becomes Kusama's sticker: an
identical mark, contributed by no one in particular, whose only power is to add to a
total. Self-obliteration performed by the checkbox is exactly right: the ego dissolved
into infinite repetition, rendered by the plainest widget the web owns (**P2** — the
consent checkbox as digital readymade; **P7** — a casual visitor cheerfully covers a room,
a literate one reads mass anonymity and *Self-Obliteration*, 1967).

The room sits on the chance/order axis (**P5**): the grid is strict order — a `repeat(auto-fill)`
lattice the layout engine lays down identically every time — while the pattern that
emerges is pure crowd entropy, no two visits coordinating their dots. And because there
is no JavaScript, there is nothing to `fetch`, nothing to store, nothing to log
(**P8**). The dry punchline is structural: reload and the room is spotless again. Like the
real gallery re-whitened for tomorrow's crowd, your obliteration was never yours to keep —
nothing you placed is recorded, and the space is restored the instant you leave (**P4** —
only change; **P9** — "please cover everything," then have it quietly undone overnight).

It answers Kusama's participatory obliteration directly, and it could not survive as a
print: it is responsive (the browser *is* the room, its dimensions variable), interactive,
and self-erasing. The mechanism — the humble consent tick, multiplied by an anonymous
crowd until the white is gone — *is* the piece.

**Reference answered:** Yayoi Kusama, *The Obliteration Room* (2002– ) and *Self-Obliteration*
(1967). **Web-native lineage:** the CSS checkbox-hack / zero-JavaScript net.craft tradition.
