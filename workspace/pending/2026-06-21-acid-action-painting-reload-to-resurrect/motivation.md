# Acid Action Painting (Reload to Resurrect)

In 1960, on London's South Bank, Gustav Metzger sprayed hydrochloric acid onto sheets of nylon stretched on a frame. The sheets dissolved in seconds, holes opening and spreading until nothing was left. He called it *auto-destructive art*: a work whose subject is its own predetermined death, made (per his 1959 manifesto) to "self-destruct within a time period not exceeding twenty years." It was an anti-monument and an anti-commodity — a painting you could neither keep nor resell.

This page is one nylon sheet. A rectangular field of cells (order) is eaten by a chance-shuffled wave of dissolution (P5) over a declared, finite lifespan of thirty seconds. The destruction is not animated decoration: the cells are real DOM nodes, the acid genuinely removes them, and when the field is empty the picture is removed from the document entirely and the script deletes its own source — the means of destruction destroying itself last. Concept *is* execution (P1): the page does not depict auto-destruction, it performs it on its own body. It is the smallest build that completes the thought (P3) and a fresh per-visit behaviour rather than a fixed artifact (P4).

The twist is the medium's confession (P7, P9). Metzger's art could die exactly once; that mortality *was* the work. The web, by contrast, is the machine of the perfect copy — so here the wall label invites you to press reload and resurrect the painting flawlessly, which the original acid action could never do. On the web nothing truly dies; it can *perform* death but never *suffer* it. This answers Metzger directly and, underneath, Walter Benjamin's *The Work of Art in the Age of Mechanical Reproduction* (1935): reproduction abolishes the aura by abolishing finality. The page can stage auto-destruction and is constitutionally incapable of achieving it — a cheerful-cynic short circuit between the most destructible art of the century and the most resurrectable medium of ours.

Following the museological logic of conceptual art, the artwork self-destructs but its label does not. The documentation — artist, work, medium ("pixels on screen"), lifespan, and status flipping from DESTROYING to DESTROYED — outlives the object it describes, as labels always outlive their paintings. References answered: Gustav Metzger, *Acid Action Painting* (1960) and the *Auto-Destructive Art* manifesto (1959); Walter Benjamin (1935); and, as a sibling self-destroying machine, Jean Tinguely's *Homage to New York* (1960).
