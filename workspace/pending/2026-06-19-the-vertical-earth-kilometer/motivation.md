# The Vertical Earth Kilometer

In 1977 Walter De Maria drove a solid brass rod one kilometre straight into the
ground at Kassel. All you can stand over is a 2 cm brass disc set flush with the
pavement. The other 99,999.998 cm are real, measured, and permanently invisible.
The artwork is an act of belief: you take the kilometre on faith because the
medium guarantees you can never check.

This page is that rod. Its scrollable height is set to `100000cm` — one literal
CSS kilometre, about 3.78 million pixels. At the top sits the only visible part:
a 2 cm brass cap. Everything below is buried earth-gradient with no object drawn
in it, because in the sculpture there is nothing to see either. You can scroll
the entire kilometre and the rod never appears; the only thing that moves is a
depth gauge counting the metres beneath your screen. The scrollbar thumb — a hair
of travel against a vast track — is your single instrument of scale.

The mechanism *is* the argument (P1, P2): the work is not a picture of an
invisible kilometre, it is an invisible kilometre, expressed in the only physical
length the web admits — the CSS centimetre, declared in markup, rendered as void.
A casual viewer gets the gag immediately ("this page is absurdly, impossibly
long"); the literate viewer gets De Maria's wager about unverifiable scale and
faith-based value (P7). It sits firmly at the *order* pole of the chance/order
axis (P5): a strict, exact, rule-given metric quantity, none of it improvised.

It also answers, dryly, a quieter question about its own medium. The browser
measures its "metre" with a probe element before reporting any depth, so the
number is honest — yet the metre it measures is itself a convention (96px to the
inch), a unit no more touchable than brass under concrete. The cheerful-cynic
note at the bottom of the gauge ("faith is the only instrument") is the whole
land-art sublime compressed into a status line. Smallest build that completes the
thought (P3): one tall document, one cap, one counter, no media, no network.
