# Non-finito

Michelangelo left the *Prigioni* — the Slaves — half-buried in their marble: torsos straining out of raw stone, a knee freed, a face still trapped. Whether by intention or abandonment, the *non-finito* became a doctrine: the figure "was always inside the stone," and the work is most alive at the instant carving stops. This page hands that decision to the browser. Six sentences (the six Prisoners) sit in a block of marble; each is set `white-space: nowrap; overflow: hidden; text-overflow: ellipsis`. The layout engine releases exactly as much of each figure as the container is wide, then carves it off with a "…". You never brought a chisel — you brought a viewport.

The mechanism **is** the argument (**P1**). The unfinished state is not drawn, illustrated, or faked with a graphic of cracked stone; it is produced live by the CSS overflow algorithm, whose literal job is to decide how much text a box may reveal. Crucially, the ellipsis glyph (U+2026) is inserted by the render engine alone: it is absent from the DOM, invisible to `textContent`, and dropped when you copy — so selecting a "half-born" line frees the whole figure that was in the marble all along. The work is therefore **dimensions variable, set by your viewport** (**P3**, smallest build that completes the thought; the minimal gesture is a single CSS property doing the sculpting). A live readout counts how many figures fully emerged versus how many remain in the stone, and roughly how many of the total letters your screen released — the number that changes every time you resize.

This belongs to the source-versus-render lineage the catalogue already runs (**narrative**, **P5** chance↔order): *Silencio* (whitespace), *La Disparition* (a stripped glyph), *Indivisible* (render-only hyphens), *Scrittura Speculare* (a mirrored render). All withhold in the render what the DOM keeps. *Non-finito* is mechanically distinct from each: it uses **truncation** — the "read more" / `line-clamp` pattern the whole web runs on — and reframes that ubiquitous act of cutting text-to-fit as Michelangelo's carving-to-stop. Order (the sentences are fixed and complete in source) meets chance (which words survive is contingent on a screen the artist never chose).

It answers Michelangelo Buonarroti's *Atlas*, *Bearded*, and *Awakening Slaves* (c. 1520–34, Galleria dell'Accademia, Florence) and, web-side, the "read more" truncation UI — the interface's promise that there is always more, deferred (**P2**, digital readymade; **P7**, double-coded: a casual reader sees a poem the machine keeps cutting off; a literate one sees the *non-finito* and the attention economy's endless truncation). The tone stays dry and cheerful-cynic (**P9**): the sentences are self-referential — the statement *about* completion is itself never completed, and the one about the browser's chisel-mark is cut, by the browser, before it can finish. Delivery is clean (**P8**): fully self-contained, no network, no cookies, no storage, CC BY-SA 4.0, attributed to Michelangelo and y-a-v-a.
