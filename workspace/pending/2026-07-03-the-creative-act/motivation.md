# The Creative Act

In 1957 Marcel Duchamp stood in Houston and told a room of artists that the work is not finished by the maker: *"The spectator brings the work in contact with the external world … and thus adds his contribution to the creative act. The creative act is not performed by the artist alone."* Every gallery since has treated this as a lovely metaphor while quietly hanging paintings that are complete whether or not anyone walks in. This piece refuses the metaphor and makes it literal machinery.

An empty framed rectangle hangs in the middle of a tall page. A rule-based composition is *only painted while the frame is inside your field of view, and only in exact proportion to how much of it you can see*. Scroll it half onto the screen and it is 44% painted; scroll it fully into view and it completes; scroll away and it un-paints back to an empty frame. The percentage is not a progress bar toward some finished object that exists elsewhere — there is no elsewhere. Off-screen, the work does not exist. The spectator is not looking at the art; the looking **is** the art being made.

The mechanism is the argument (**P1**). `IntersectionObserver` reports `intersectionRatio` — the precise fraction of an element inside the viewport — which is, read literally, *how much of the work the spectator has brought into contact with the world*. That number drives how many strokes are drawn. Duchamp's sentence and the browser API turn out to describe the same quantity, so the API is not illustrating the idea, it is the idea. It is a **digital readymade** (**P2**): a surveillance-and-analytics primitive (built to measure whether an ad is "viewable") re-pointed at the oldest question about who finishes a picture. Because it is a behaviour that regenerates and reverses per glance rather than a fixed artifact (**P4**), and because the composition itself is a small per-visit order-system of squares, circles, bars and crosses (**P5**, order), it reads two ways at once (**P7**): a casual visitor gets the gag — *look away and the painting leaves* — while a literate one gets the fifty-year argument about the spectator completing the creative act.

It answers Duchamp's *The Creative Act* (1957) directly, and shakes hands with Umberto Eco's *The Open Work* (1962): the "open" work completed by its receiver, here made closed the instant you close your eyes. A print cannot do this — a print is fully painted in an empty room, which is exactly the assumption this work will not grant. Only a medium that can *watch itself being watched* can hold Duchamp to his word. Nothing is stored, nothing is sent; the only record of your contribution to the creative act is the fact that, for as long as you looked, it was there.
