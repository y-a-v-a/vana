# Cartel

A monochrome painting hangs framed on a warm gallery wall. Beneath it, where the museum tombstone should be, there is a brushed-metal **label plate — and it is blank**. Everything a label carries — title, artist, date, medium, dimensions, edition, price — lives *only* inside the native `title`-attribute tooltip. To read it you must rest your pointer on the work and wait out the delay the operating system alone controls; the OS then draws the caption in the OS's own font, for as long as the OS decides, and takes it away again. The label is never in the render tree, never on the canvas, and cannot be styled, screenshotted in the same frame, or reached at all on a touch device — where the artwork simply has no label.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). In a museum the wall label mediates everything: we read the caption before we look, and the institution's authority speaks through it. Here the medium withholds that caption and hands its timing, typography, and very existence to the browser/OS. The context that grants the object its status is deferred, fugitive, and not the artist's to design — institutional critique enacted by a piece of native browser chrome (**P2**, the tooltip as digital readymade). The pun sits in the title: a *cartel* is both the French word for a wall label and a body that fixes value; the caption that carries "Price on request" is exactly the thing you can never quite hold still.

It answers the museum **cartel / "tombstone"** convention directly, and stands beside **Joseph Kosuth's *Titled (Art as Idea as Idea)*** (where the label-as-definition *is* the work) and **Marcel Broodthaers's** fictional museum labels, *"Ceci n'est pas un objet d'art"* (1968–72). It sits on the chance↔order axis (**P5**) with a per-visit `Math.random` hue held only in a closure, and reports the live viewport as its "dimensions," so no two visits are captioned the same (**P4**).

The build is the smallest that completes the thought (**P3**): one framed div, one empty plate, one tooltip string, no libraries, no storage, no network. Double-coded (**P7**): the surface gag — *a painting whose caption you can only get by hovering and waiting, and never on your phone* — lands without wall text, while the literate reading is a note on who owns context. The visible page colophon states the license and attribution honestly; the artwork's own diegetic cartel stays where the museum keeps it — just out of reach.
