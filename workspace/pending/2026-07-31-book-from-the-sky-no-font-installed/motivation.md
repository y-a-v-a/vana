# Book from the Sky (□ No Font Installed)

Xu Bing spent four years hand-carving four thousand invented Chinese characters — glyphs that look completely legible and mean absolutely nothing — and printed them as *Book from the Sky* (天書, 1987–1991): a text that withholds meaning by design. The web already runs the inverse machine, silently, everywhere. Feed a browser a real, meaningful character from a script whose font it does not possess and it prints □ — the `.notdef` glyph, the box that Google's Noto project was explicitly named to abolish ("**no** more **tofu**"). Meaning is fully present — in the source, on the clipboard, in memory — and yet unreadable, not because the sign is empty like Xu Bing's, but because *your particular device lacks the key*. This piece is the browser's own Book from the Sky, one that Xu Bing didn't have to carve.

The mechanism **is** the argument (**P1**). The page hands each rare Unicode block — Ogham, Gothic, Linear B, Cuneiform, Egyptian Hieroglyphs, Tangut — to the browser's generic `serif` fallback chain and lets the font stack decide what survives. A canvas routine then interrogates the medium directly: it rasterises each script's first codepoint and compares the bitmap against a codepoint guaranteed to have no glyph (U+10FFFF). Identical pixels mean the browser drew tofu; different pixels mean a font was found. The result is a per-device readout — "you can read 3 of 11 scripts" — that no author wrote and no two visitors necessarily share. It is a **digital readymade** (**P2**): the `.notdef` glyph and the font-fallback algorithm are the found objects, recontextualised as a work about legibility and access.

This is Walter Benjamin stood on his head. Benjamin worried that mechanical reproduction strips the aura of the original; here reproduction is perfect and lossless — every codepoint is transmitted intact — and the loss happens at the *receiver*, whose installed fonts decide how much of the artwork exists for them at all (**P4**, per-visit, per-device, minted now and stored nowhere). The surface gag reads instantly to any visitor — exotic glyphs, or a page of neat empty boxes, with a scoreboard (**P7**, **P9**): the dry joke that a "book" can be blank for you and full for the person beside you. Underneath sits the real point about who gets to read, whose scripts ship with the operating system, and the quiet violence of the box that means *no glyph*.

It answers Xu Bing directly, and stakes out territory the catalogue's neighbours leave open: *COPY / COPY* concerns homoglyphs that look identical but differ in bytes; this concerns real bytes that render as nothing at all — the opposite failure. *The Most Reproduced Image* is about each engine's broken-**image** icon; this is about each device's font **coverage**. And it holds the y-a-v-a ethics without compromise (**P8**): CC BY 4.0, attribution to Xu Bing and Benjamin, no cookies, no storage, and — fittingly for a work about what the network can and cannot deliver to you — not a single network request.
