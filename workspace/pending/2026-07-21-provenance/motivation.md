# Provenance

A masterpiece is, in commercial fact, a chain of receipts. Its price is underwritten less by the paint than by the names of everyone who has owned it — the ledger of custody that a museum prints on the wall in tiny type. *Provenance* is a museum wall panel for a web document. It reads the browser's own custody metadata — `history.length` (how many times the session has changed hands) and `document.referrer` (the immediate prior owner) — and renders a proper provenance list of exactly the right length, with every owner blacked out. You can count the transfers; you can never name them. The "Transfer to a new owner" button calls `history.pushState`, so the work changes hands again — no navigation, no network, no record of who now holds it — and a new redacted line joins the ledger.

The mechanism *is* the argument (**P1**). Provenance is precisely a chain of custody, and the web's only automatic chain of custody is the browser's history and referrer chain — which privacy engineering (Referrer-Policy, session isolation) has deliberately gutted. So the referrer arrives blank and the history entries are anonymous integers: the browser counts the owners but, being decent, refuses to name them. The redaction bars are not a graphic effect painted over data; there is no data. The blackness is the honest shape of what the privacy web will disclose. This makes the work perfectly authentic and completely unprovenanced at once — which, as the trade has always maintained, is indistinguishable from a fake (**P6** value/authenticity; **P7** double-coding: a comically empty museum panel for the casual viewer, an argument about aura and the market for the literate one).

It answers **Hans Haacke's** provenance pieces — *Manet-PROJEKT ’74* and *Seurat’s “Les Poseuses” (small version) 1888–1975* — which presented the full owner-by-owner, price-by-price history of a single painting as the artwork, and were duly censored by the institutions that owned the apparatus. Here the apparatus is the browser, and it censors the panel for the opposite, sympathetic reason: your privacy. It also answers **Duchamp's** readymade (the found object is the document's custody log) and **Walter Benjamin**, who located a work's aura in its "presence in time and space… the history to which it has been subject" — the very history the web is built to forget.

It lives only in a browser (**P4**, **G1**): a print cannot count its own owners or lose their names on reload. The privacy stance is not a disclaimer bolted on — it is the material (**P8**). No cookies, no trackers, no requests, nothing stored; reload mints a fresh object with no past, and the market weeps.
