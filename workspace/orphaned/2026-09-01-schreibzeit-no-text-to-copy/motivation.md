# Schreibzeit (No Text to Copy)

Hanne Darboven filled thousands of pages by hand with numbers — running enumerations and the summed digits of dates, her *Konstruktionen* — not to say anything with them but to spend time, to make writing itself the material. She called one of the largest works *Schreibzeit*: writing-time. The labour was the point; the number was only its residue.

This page lets the browser do it instead. Every cell in the grid is an **empty** element — `<i></i>` with nothing inside. The visible column of ascending numbers is produced entirely by CSS `counter-increment` and `content: counter(n)` in the `::before` layer. The layout engine performs Darboven's lifetime of enumeration in a single pass, at no cost, and — this is the whole gesture — it leaves **no text**. Select the page and copy it: you paste blank cells. View source: empty tags. As a document, this dense construction is zero characters long. The small line at the foot reads `document.textContent.length` back to you to prove it, then reports that the first cell's rendered number exists only where `getComputedStyle(cell, '::before')` can see it.

This enacts **P1** — concept *is* execution: generated content is, by specification, not part of the document's text, so a construction that looks entirely written is, as writing, absent. The mechanism *is* the argument, not an illustration of it. It is a **P2** digital readymade (the CSS counter, the web's own machine for automatic numbering) built at the **P3** minimum: essentially one CSS rule, one optional JS attestation, no images, no fonts, no network. It reads at two depths (**P7**): a wall of numbers for the casual eye, a precise note on the render/text split for the literate one.

It answers Darboven directly and, through her, **Walter Benjamin**: mechanical reproduction here does not merely copy the labour — it deletes the *Zeit* from *Schreibzeit*. The hand's accumulated time is exactly what the browser cannot reproduce, because it never spent any. Where On Kawara's dates and Roman Opałka's single climbing integer are about the *number* (memory, mortality), this is about the *writing* — a text that is all surface and no text layer, uncopyable because, in the only sense a machine keeps, it was never written.
